I

Chapter one

Where heraldry comes from

A medieval invention born of a very practical need: recognising a man armoured from head to toe.

I.1

A sign to recognise the knight

118 words

Heraldry is the system of coloured figures, borne first on the shield, that identify a person, a family or a community and are passed on according to fixed rules. It appears in Western Europe in the 12th century, in the area between the Loire and the Rhine as well as in England.

The best-established material cause is the evolution of armour: the helm closes and covers the face, and the mail hauberk makes all silhouettes alike. On the battlefield as at the tournament, a knight can no longer be recognised. So a stable design is painted on the shield and repeated on the banner — the ancestor of the emblem handed down from generation to generation.

Source: Joubert (1977) · Pastoureau (1979)
I.2

Tournament, seal, crusade: what created it, what spread it

53 words

The tournament, a codified martial sport from the 1120s and 1130s, made emblems legible at a distance a general need. The seal, affixed to deeds, fixed and published these figures. These two uses — one military and sporting, the other legal — explain the birth of the system better than holy war alone.

Source: Pastoureau (1979)
State of knowledge

Established: heraldry spread widely in Europe between 1120 and 1160; its first dated figures appear on seals and in tournament accounts of this period.

Debated: the common idea of a heraldry “invented by the Crusades” is now qualified. The Crusades mainly spread and internationalised a system already forming in the West. Stories tracing a given coat of arms back to a specific crusader are most often family legend, not proof.

The shield and its points

Heraldry is always read from the point of view of the person bearing the shield — and so the reverse of ours.

II.1

The shape of the shield

60 words

The support of a coat of arms is the shield (écu). Its shape makes no difference to the blazon: it follows fashion and country — the early triangular shield, the “modern” French shield with its point, the lozenge traditionally reserved for women, the notched targe, the oval ecclesiastical cartouche. The drawing may vary; the arms remain the same.

Source: Joubert (1977)
II.2

Shapes by country — and the caution they call for

230 words

The same coat of arms can be drawn within many outlines. Books arrange them by country, but these labels should be handled with care.

  • Tournament, or “banner” shield: a simple quadrilateral, seven parts wide by eight high.
  • Italian: shaped like a chamfron, also called a horse's head (testa di cavallo). Another Italian shield, oval, was used by clergy and, in France, by married women.
  • Swiss: three points at the top. English: angular, the top extended into horizontal horns. German: a tournament targe, notched to rest the lance.
  • Polish: with multiple notches; the English shield distinguishes a 16th-century state, with ears and a rounded point, and another from the 17th.
  • With a rounded lower edge: Spain, Portugal and Flanders, which were long Spanish.
  • Hungarian: in the 14th century, round, triangular and oval shields, and a hexagonal one attested at Gelence; the tárcsapajzs, an elongated rectangle with a round notch in one corner for the lance; the doborpajzs, rounded at the bottom so as not to hurt the horse's neck.

These national names are misleading: the Hungarian source notes that these shapes spread almost everywhere in Europe at the same time, so that the shape only dates a coat of arms as “from” a given period — a baroque shield can't precede the baroque. None of this enters the blazon.

Source: Wikipedia (2026)
State of knowledge

Read at the source: the names and descriptions above, from the four articles cited.

Drawn here: the Atelier offers these shapes, traced by the encyclopedia from these descriptions and the reference figures; they are approximate outlines, fitted to a common box, not copies. The “seven by eight” proportions of the tournament shield are therefore not respected.

Not verified: which shapes were used for papal arms. The “horse's head” is the Italian shield; it isn't attributed here to any particular pope.

II.3

Dexter and sinister: the vocabulary trap

70 words

All positions are named from the bearer's point of view, not the viewer's. Dexter (the knight's right) is therefore on our left when we look at the shield; sinister is on our right. The top is called the chief, the bottom the base, the centre the heart (the exact central point, the fess point, abîme). Confusing the two sides is the most common beginner's mistake.

Source: Joubert (1977)

Tinctures: metals, colours, furs

Two metals, five colours, two furs — and a rule that governs almost everything.

III.1

The nine classic tinctures

62 words

The hues of heraldry are called tinctures (émaux). They fall into three families: the metals (or and argent), the colours (gules, azure, sable, vert, purpure) and the furs (ermine and vair, with their variants). Each box below shows the tincture in colour, then its rendering in engraving — the system of hatching that indicates a colour in black and white.

Source: Joubert (1977)
III.2

The hatching system

66 words

This hatching code was fixed in the 17th century (it is attributed to the Jesuit Silvester Petra Sancta, around 1638) to engrave coats of arms without losing the colour information: or dotted, argent left bare, gules hatched with vertical lines, azure with horizontal lines, sable with crossed lines, vert and purpure with opposing diagonals. It is purely conventional — a notation, not a protected work.

III.3

The rule of tincture

103 words

A fundamental rule, the rule of tincture forbids placing metal on metal or colour on colour: or isn't set on argent, nor gules on azure. A figure in a colour must rest on a field of metal, and vice versa. Its purpose is legibility at a distance — the very object of heraldry.

Exceptions exist and are so striking that they are called “enquire” arms (armes à enquerre, “arms to ask about”): the deliberate breach of the rule marks out special arms. The classic example is the Kingdom of Jerusalem, argent, a cross potent or — metal on metal, on purpose.

Source: Joubert (1977)

Chapter IV

The divisions of the field

Dividing the field with lines — their direction, shape and number make up a whole language.

In this chapterIV.1 Dividing rather than chargingIV.2 Lines: the shape of the edgeIV.3 Multiple divisions and further divisions
IV.1

Dividing rather than charging

87 words

Before placing figures, the field can be divided by one or more lines. Simple divisions take their name from the line that creates them: per pale (vertical line; parti), per fess (horizontal; coupé), per bend and per bend sinister (the two diagonals; tranché and taillé). Combining them gives quarterly (four quarters; écartelé) or per saltire (écartelé en sautoir).

The rule of tincture relaxes on a line of division: two colours may touch there, because they are juxtaposed rather than superimposed.

Source: Joubert (1977)
IV.2

Lines: the shape of the edge

144 words

A line of division isn't necessarily straight. Its shape is blazoned, and each profile has a name that multiplies the vocabulary accordingly — a line drawn differently is enough to tell two families apart. The main ones:

Wavy (ondé): in gentle waves. Embattled (crénelé): with square crenellations, like the top of a wall. Indented (denché): in large triangular teeth (dancetty, dancetté, has only three or four, very prominent). Engrailed (engrêlé): edged with small arcs whose points bite outwards; invected (cannelé, or invecté): the same arcs, reversed. Nebuly (nébulé): in rounded scallops suggesting clouds. Vivré: in a sharp zigzag.

The same adjective applies to the edges of ordinaries: a fess wavy, a bend engrailed, a cross indented. The line alone becomes a mark of identity.

Source: Joubert (1977) · Wenzler (2015)
IV.3

Multiple divisions and further divisions

133 words

Beyond divisions in two, the field can be shared into more parts. Tierced (tiercé) cuts it into three equal parts of three tinctures — in pale (vertically), in fess (horizontally) or in bend (diagonally). Gyronny (gironné) brings together triangles (usually eight, called gyrons) that meet at the heart of the shield.

Above all, divisions can be combined: a field already divided per fess can have each of its parts divided per pale, and vice versa. “Per fess and per pale” amounts to quarterly; “per pale, with two lines per fess” gives six quarters; and so on up to the great quartered shields that set side by side the arms of several allied houses. It is through this play of divisions that composite arms tell a genealogy.

Source: Joubert (1977) · Wenzler (2015)

The rules of heraldry

Heraldry isn't random decoration: it is a grammar of lines, divisions and outlines, each variant of which has a name. Joubert makes it the heart of his introduction — here are his rules, on blank shields.

V.1

Three rules before anything else

151 words

Even before dividing or charging a field, heraldry obeys three simple rules that Joubert places at the foundation of his introduction:

  • The rule of tincture (see chapter III): never metal on metal, never colour on colour. It governs charges and divisions alike, with the exception of the lines of division themselves, where two colours may be juxtaposed.
  • The direction of the blazon (see chapter II): everything is described from the point of view of the shield's bearer — dexter and sinister are reversed relative to the viewer.
  • The order of description (see chapter VIII): the field, then the main ordinary or charge, then the secondary figures — always in this order, never improvised.

This chapter dwells on a fourth family of rules, less often summed up in one place: that of the drawing of the line — the line that divides the field or edges an ordinary — and its combinations.

Source: Joubert (1977) · Pastoureau (1979)
V.2

The simple line: one line, four directions

84 words

A line (trait) divides the field in two. Its direction, and that alone, gives the division its name: vertical for per pale, horizontal for per fess, diagonal from dexter chief to sinister base for per bend, the opposite diagonal — from sinister chief to dexter base — for per bend sinister. V.3

Dividing further: from the simple division to the great quarterly

141 words

A line can be multiplied or crossed. Multiplying a horizontal line beyond one gives tierced in fess (two lines, three pieces), then, taken to an even number of equal bands, barry or barruly (burelé). The same logic vertically leads from per pale to tierced in pale and then paly; diagonally, from per bend to bendy, from per bend sinister to bendy sinister.

Crossing, strictly speaking, means overlaying two lines of different directions: per fess and per pale together give quarterly (four quarters); the four lines — per pale, per fess, per bend, per bend sinister — crossed at the same point give gyronny, whose eight triangles (gyrons) meet at the heart of the shield. It is by crossing ever further that great family shields add up their alliances, quarter after quarter.

V.4

The shape of the line: curved and toothed lines

183 words

Regardless of its direction, a line can stop being straight: its shape is then blazoned as an adjective, and applies to a line of division as well as to the edge of an ordinary (a fess, a bend, a cross). Joubert gives the following series, all shown here on a simple per fess to compare the same line in eight forms:

  • Wavy (ondé) — in continuous, rounded waves.
  • Engrailed (engrêlé) — edged with small round points, the hollows facing outwards.
  • Invected (cannelé, or invecté) — the same arc as engrailed, but reversed: the points turn into the field.
  • Indented (denché) — in many regular triangular teeth, like a saw blade.
  • Dancetty (dancetté) — the same tooth, but broad and few (three or four well-marked projections).
  • Embattled (crénelé) — with square crenellations, like the top of a wall.
  • Nebuly (nébulé) — in rounded scallops alternating bump and hollow, in the manner of the conventional clouds of medieval drawing.
  • Vivré — in a sharp zigzag, with re-entrant and salient angles.

V.5

Couped: when the ordinary no longer touches the edge

142 words

An honourable ordinary — cross, saltire, bend, pale, fess — is drawn by default running to the edge of the shield and cut off by its outline, as a line of division is. When it is instead shortened and floats in the middle of the field, without touching the edges, it is called couped (alésé). The distinction isn't cosmetic: it changes the blazon (“cross couped” isn't “cross”) and therefore the identity of the arms.

Two neighbouring notions, not to be confused: an issuant (issant) figure shows only its upper part, as if emerging from a line; a figure said to be issuing (mouvant) from a point of the shield (the chief, a flank) starts there without occupying its full length. Couping, for its part, shortens the ordinary at both ends, detaching it from all the edges.

State of knowledge

The names of lines (wavy, indented, engrailed…) and the notion of a couped ordinary belong to the standard technical vocabulary, stable since the classic treatises. Two points remain subject to variation: the boundary between indented and dancetty (a matter of proportion, not of nature, and not all books set the same threshold) and the exact number of lines at which a multiplication of bands becomes barruly/paly/bendy rather than a named tierced — common usage puts the limit at around six to eight pieces, with no absolute rule.

VI.1

Reinforcements turned emblems

109 words

The honourable ordinaries (pièces honorables) are the large geometric figures that cross the shield. Many are said to derive from the painted or metal reinforcements of the early shield — the fess (horizontal band), the pale (vertical), the chevron, the bend and bend sinister (the diagonals), the chief (upper third), the cross, the saltire, the bordure and the orle.

Reduced in width, they give diminutives (Joubert calls them rebattements; common usage keeps that word for repetition, see below): the narrowed fess becomes a barrulet, the pale a pallet, the bend a bendlet. Multiplied, they give rhythm to the field.

Source: Joubert (1977)
VI.2

Honourable or ordinary: a shifting boundary

291 words

An ordinary (pièce) is a geometric figure, bounded by geometric lines, that occupies a fixed place on the shield. The simplest and oldest are called honourable; the others are called ordinary or “of the second order” (subordinaries). The boundary shifts a great deal: authors agree neither on the list nor on the criterion behind it.

Only one thing is agreed. Five ordinaries are disputed by nobody: the fess, the chief, the bend, the pale and the chevron. The bend sinister — the mirror of the bend, but rarer — and the bordure very often complete the list. To go further, three criteria have been proposed: surface (an honourable ordinary is wide enough to bear charges, about a third of the shield), attachment (it touches at least two edges of the shield, a memory of the reinforcements fixed to it) and the ability to be repeated so as to cover the field (see the next article). Each adds or removes figures.

The authors' lists show this. To the five undisputed ordinaries, they add:

  • A. O'Kelly de Galway (1901): the cross and the saltire;
  • Amédée de Foras (1883): the bend sinister, the cross and the saltire;
  • L.-A. Duhoux d'Argicourt (1896): the bend sinister, the cross, the saltire, the quarter, the gyron and the champagne;
  • Jouffroy d'Eschavannes (1855): the same, plus the escutcheon at the heart — with the reservation that the champagne only counts since Napoleon.

None of this changes the way of blazoning: one writes “argent, a fess gules” whether the fess is held to be honourable or not. It is a vocabulary of classification, not of grammar. The list of ten figures that opens this chapter is Joubert's; it is far from unanimous.

Source: Wikipedia (2026)
State of knowledge

Established: the definition of the ordinary, the five undisputed ordinaries, and the fact that the honourable / ordinary distinction is “very fluid” and affects neither the creation nor the reading of arms.

Debated: everything else — the exact list, the criterion. The four authors are cited from Wikipedia; their works have not been read.

VI.3

Repeating: from the ordinary to the striped field

314 words

A simple division or ordinary is repeated (rebattu) when it recurs across the whole field: this is the rebattement. Start from per pale or and gules. A first repetition gives three zones; it is no longer a division but an ordinary: “or, a pale gules”. Each further repetition brings up now an ordinary, now a division, according to the number of zones:

  • an odd number of zones (three, five, seven…) leaves the same tincture at both edges: ordinaries are blazoned — “or, three pales gules” — up to three, exceptionally four;
  • an even number gives edges of opposite tinctures: this is an alternating field, blazoned by the type of stripe and the number of pieces — “paly of eight or and gules”.

The type of stripe follows the direction of the line: paly (vertical), barry (horizontal; fascé), bendy, bendy sinister, chevronny. When the pieces are many — beyond eight — and thin, they change name in French: vergeté, burelé (barruly), coticé (coticé en barre, or contre-coticé, in the other direction), étayé for chevrons. Stripes of large pieces have six by default — nine for three tinctures — which goes unsaid; beyond that, it is stated: “barry of eight argent and gules”. Stripes of small pieces have ten by default (twelve for three tinctures), but the exact number no longer matters there. In the Atelier, the “Rayé” (striped) field gives these stripes, from four to twelve pieces — or, with an odd number of zones, from two to six repeated pieces (“or, three pales gules”).

A vocabulary trap: the French word pièce has two senses here. Fascé de six pièces counts zones; honourable pièces are geometric figures with a fixed place (see the previous article). To distinguish them, some French writers use a small letter for a number, and a capital for a shape.

Source: Wikipedia (2026)
One word, two senses

This site's glossary gives rebattement, after Joubert, the sense of diminutive: the narrowed fess becomes a barrulet. The Wikipedia article “Rebattement” keeps the word for repetition, notes that authors define it in very different ways, that the notion seems archaic and that the term, less and less used, isn't part of the blazon. This chapter says “repeat” for repetition and “diminish” for narrowing (next article); both usages are found in books.

VI.4

Diminishing: a whole family from the fess

291 words

An ordinary can also be diminished: its width is reduced, and its name changes. The language of heraldry thus draws, from a single ordinary, a family of related figures.

  • The fess gives the divise (alone on its field), the burelle or barrulet (in even numbers, four or more: “argent, six barrulets gules”), the trangle (the same, in odd numbers), the jumelle or bar gemel (two barrulets separated by a space equal to their width, the whole as thick as a fess) and the tierce (three trangles, on the same principle).
  • The pale gives the vergette or pallet (repeated five times or more).
  • The bend gives the cotice or bendlet (reduced to a third, or repeated beyond four), then the filet (a very narrow bendlet). The baton (bâton) and the traverse, reduced bends or bendlets, serve mainly as marks of difference (see the chapter “Differencing”).
  • The chevron gives the chevronel (chevronnel), or étai.
  • The chief gives the comble, or chef retrait.
  • The bordure gives the filière; the cross, the filet en croix (étrez); the champagne, the plaine.

The definitions float, even within one list. The divise is sometimes a fess reduced by half, sometimes one reduced to less than a third of its width; its default place is that of the chief for Charles de Grandmaison, that of the fess for Pierre Palliot. The comble is a chief reduced to two thirds of its height (sometimes to half) in one entry, to less than half in another. As for the baton, authors give three definitions of it. Keep the logic — same ordinary, narrower, another name — rather than the measurements, which the blazon doesn't require.

Source: Wikipedia (2026)
VI.5

The chevron, the all-purpose ordinary

249 words

The chevron is formed of two branches sloping diagonally on either side of the vertical axis, meeting at a point: an inverted V, like the fusion of a half-bend and a half-bend sinister. It normally sits with its point upwards, its branches touching the edges of the shield; the point is usually at the honour point — formerly, at times, up to the top of the field — and each branch doesn't exceed two sevenths of the width of the shield.

Its shape takes up a good part of the field, which limits its repetition: three at most, rarely more. A field covered with an even number of alternating chevrons is called chevronny (“chevronny of twelve argent and gules”).

Narrow, it becomes a chevronel — reduced to a third or a quarter of its width — or an étai, which most authors hold to be a synonym. Only one, L.-A. Duhoux d'Argicourt, sees in it a small chevron that “supports” another charge; the Wikipedia article considers this reading a minority, even isolated, one, and recalls that in practice the widths of chevron, chevronel and étai vary so much that the three names are interchangeable.

Finally, the chevron takes many attributes: abaissé (set lower than usual), renversé or versé (point down), couché (turned a quarter turn), écimé (its point cut off horizontally), couped (alésé, its feet not touching the edge), failli (one branch missing), en ogive (curved branches); and its edges can be embattled, wavy, indented, engrailed…

State of knowledge

VI.6

Three corner squares: canton, franc-canton, quarter

201 words

Three square figures sit in a corner of the shield, in dexter chief unless stated otherwise: the canton, the franc-canton and the quarter (franc-quartier). They differ only in size — the canton is the smallest, the quarter the largest — and authors disagree on the measurements. Wikipedia's list of ordinaries gives, as values quite often found: two sevenths of the width of the shield for the canton, a third for the franc-canton (a ninth of the surface), half for the quarter (a quarter of the surface).

The word canton has another sense: it also refers to the four regions of the field left visible by a cross or a saltire — hence a cross “cantoned” by four charges.

The franc-canton and the quarter were much used in Imperial heraldry to set the bearer's title and office in the shield (see the article “1808 — rank enters the shield”), often with confusion: a quarter was reduced to the size of a franc-canton — and yet blazoned as “a quarter of a ninth of the shield” — so that the figures of the lower layers would stay legible. The name is therefore improper, as the chapter on law already notes.

VI.7

The top and bottom of the shield: chief, champagne, pile, point

228 words

The chief occupies the upper third of the shield; the champagne, the lower third. Diminished, the champagne becomes a plaine; stepped, it is called pignonnée. It shouldn't be confused with the terrace, a charge often issuing from the base, whose upper edge is irregular. In coats of arms forming a picture, the champagne plays the role that the terrace plays in the external ornaments.

The chief can be lowered (it is then surmounted by its own chief), supported by an ordinary, or reduced: the comble, or chef retrait. Reduced to a third of its height, it would be rompu — a term that Amédée de Foras says is not used, the examples found resulting from a misreading.

The chief combines with other ordinaries: the chef-pal (T-shaped), the chef-bande and the chef-barre (seven-shaped) are the main ones; others, like the chef-chevron, are very rare, or even unused.

Two triangles also occupy the top or bottom. The pile is an isosceles triangle whose base, two thirds of the shield's width, hangs from the chief and whose point aims downwards without reaching the bottom. The point (pointe), as an ordinary, is the same triangle reversed: base at the bottom, apex towards the chief without reaching it. Bendwise or bendwise sinister, the pile and the point issue from a corner of the shield.

Source: Wikipedia (2026)
VI.8

Rare ordinaries: pall, gyron, gusset, hamaide, flank…

328 words

The lists of ordinaries include figures seldom met, but whose names are worth knowing.

  • The pall (pairle) is formed of the lower part of the pale and the upper halves of the bend and bend sinister, joined at the centre: a Y. Shortened and narrowed (“couped and pointed”), it corresponds to the shakefork of British heraldry, which has no name of its own in French.
  • The gyron (giron) is a right-angled triangle whose base is half of one side of the field and whose opposite angle is at the centre of the shield; by default, it issues from the upper half of the dexter flank. Its shapes are those of a gyronny of eight.
  • The gusset (gousset) is composed, at the top, of a triangle issuing from the chief, which joins a pale below. It takes its name from a piece of cloth, then from the metal piece that linked the front and back of a suit of armour.
  • The hamaide is formed of three couped fesses with rounded or chamfered ends. It is held to be one single ordinary, so that “a hamaide” is more correct than “three hamaides”. Its name comes from La Hamaide, in Belgium, whose lords bore or, a hamaide gules.
  • The flank (flanc) corresponds to the dexter or sinister edge of the shield and extends to a third of it. The French word adextré is ambiguous (a secondary ordinary to the dexter of a main one, or a shield whose dexter third changes tincture): “argent, a dexter flank gules” is preferred.
  • The escarre (carpenter's square) forms a border on only two sides of a quarter.
  • The escutcheon (écusson) is almost unanimously held to be an honourable ordinary when set at the fess point, a third of the width and height of the shield; in numbers or elsewhere, it is merely a charge, without changing name — as the cross becomes a small cross.

Chapter VII

Charges

VII.1

Natural, artificial, chimerical

83 words

All figures placed on the field apart from the honourable ordinaries are called charges (meubles). They fall into three families: natural charges (lion, eagle, fish, rose, oak, heavenly bodies), artificial ones (tower, sword, key, wheel, ship) and chimerical ones (dragon, griffin, unicorn, double-headed eagle). A single charge comes in countless variants: its position, its parts in another colour, its attributes — everything is stated, nothing is implied. It is this vocabulary of posture and detail that makes the richness of heraldry.

Source: Joubert (1977)
VII.2

Three tiers of charges

144 words

Since any object can become a charge, an exhaustive list would tend towards infinity. The French reference list gets round this with a criterion worth repeating, because it says something about the nature of heraldry. It keeps only three kinds of figure:

  • those that don't exist outside heraldry — the chess rook, the mascle, the escarbuncle, the martlet. Pure inventions of heraldry, they have no referent but themselves;
  • those whose depiction is so codified that it has broken away from its model — the French “leopard”, the nettle leaf, the horse-headed panther spitting flames;
  • those that are perfectly recognisable but so often used that they can't be left out — the tower, the sword, the key.

The first tier is the most interesting and least known: these are figures that can't be seen anywhere else, since they represent nothing. The gallery begins with them.

Source: Wikipedia (2026)
VII.3

The lion: attitude makes the blazon

181 words

The lion is the most widespread charge in all European heraldry — the animal of courage and sovereignty. It is described by its attitude, not its species. Standing in profile on one hind leg, one foreleg raised, it is rampant: its default position, so common that it isn't always even stated. Walking horizontally, it is passant; if it walks and turns its head towards the viewer, it becomes, in French, a leopard (léopard; lion passant guardant in English) — not another animal, but the same one in another pose.

The details are blazoned separately: the lion is armed when its claws are a different tincture, langued (lampassé) when its tongue is, crowned, pizzled (vilené). Its tail, finally, is read separately — raised, forked (fourchée, double), knotted (nouée) or passed between the legs. Two lions one above the other are called leopards by some, lions passant by others according to tradition: this is a point where the English and French schools diverge, and where one must remain cautious.

Source: Joubert (1977) · Pastoureau (1979)
VII.4

The eagle: the bird of power

128 words

After the lion comes the eagle, an emblem of empire inherited from Rome. Its usual position is displayed (éployée; wings spread on either side, head in profile towards dexter) — so constant that it is often left unsaid. The wings can be lowered or raised, the feathers counted. Like the lion, the eagle takes details in another tincture: beaked, membered (the legs), langued.

Two heads facing away give the double-headed eagle, the sign of empires claiming a double inheritance (Holy Roman Empire, Russia, Byzantium). A small eagle is called an eaglet (aiglette); scattered in large numbers, alerions — eaglets without beak or legs — sometimes fill the whole field. The bird everywhere keeps the same symbolic charge: height, vigilance, dominion.

Source: Joubert (1977)
VII.5

The rest of the bestiary

143 words

Beyond the lion and eagle, the heraldic ark is vast. Among the quadrupeds: the bear, the stag (called ramé for its antlers, élancé when it leaps), the boar, the wolf, the horse (gai if without harness, effrayé if it rears), the dog. They borrow the same attitudes as the lion: passant, rampant, salient, courant.

Among the birds, the martlet (merlette) — a stylised blackbird without beak or legs — often serves as a mark of cadency; the cock (hardi, crêté, barbé) belatedly became a French national emblem; the dove, raven and swan complete the aviary. The waters provide the dolphin (curved, emblem of the Dauphiné) and various fish; the world of small creatures, the bee and the butterfly. Each beast trails a procession of meanings inherited from the medieval bestiary.

Source: Joubert (1977)
VII.6

Chimerical creatures

89 words

The medieval imagination filled shields with beasts that don't exist. The griffin combines the front of an eagle with the back of a lion — the two sovereigns in one. The dragon (winged, four-legged) and its variant the wyvern (guivre; two legs, serpent's tail) guard treasures and gates. The unicorn, a horse with a single horn, stands for purity; the Melusine, a serpent-woman, haunts the arms of certain houses. These figures are blazoned like the others: by their posture and the tinctures of their parts.

Source: Joubert (1977)
VII.7

The great family of the cross

118 words

The cross is at once an honourable ordinary (when it touches the edges) and a charge (when it floats in the field), and it multiplies into dozens of variants according to the shape of its arms. The commonest: the cross formy or pattée (arms flaring at the ends), the cross moline or anchored cross (ancrée, ending in anchor flukes), the cross potent (T-shaped), the cross flory (fleurdelisée, fleurs-de-lis at the ends), the cross crosslet (recroisettée, small crosses at the ends), not forgetting the cross of Toulouse (clechy, voided and pommetty) or the cross of Lorraine (with a double crossbar). Naming the cross precisely already places arms in a region and a history.

Source: Joubert (1977)
VII.8

Named crosses: Jerusalem, Saint Andrew, the East

295 words

Some crosses are identified not by the shape of their arms but by a proper name, that of a place, a saint or a Church. The name then serves as a complete description.

  • The cross of Jerusalem is a cross potent — each arm ends in a T — between four small crosses. The arms given to the Kingdom of Jerusalem, argent, a cross potent or between four small crosses of the same, set or on argent: the most cited example of “enquire” arms.
  • The cross with a double crossbar is called archiepiscopal or patriarchal: it marks an archbishop. The one now called the cross of Lorraine first bore the name cross of Anjou.
  • The cross of Saint Andrew is simply the saltire, two bends crossing diagonally.
  • The Orthodox cross charges a couped pale with three crossbars, the first and third narrower, the third sloping to sinister; the Russian cross keeps only two, the lower sloping to sinister.
  • The calvary cross isn't a cross but a picture: three couped and raised crosses, the middle one larger than the two flanking it.

Other common names refer to shapes recognised at a glance: Latin cross (the foot longer than the other three arms), Maltese, Huguenot, Celtic, of Saint James, and arms ending in a fleur-de-lis (flory or florencée), in a trefoil (botonny; tréflée), in a ball (pommelly; pommée), in a fork (fourchée) or in a point (fitchy; fichée). The gallery of charges shows them one by one.

State of knowledge — The symbolic meaning given to each cross (virtues, events of the Passion, religious orders) belongs to another literature, often without verifiable sources; it isn't repeated here. Only the names and shapes are given.

Source: Wikipedia (2026)
VII.9

Heavenly bodies and round figures

134 words

The sky provides its figures. The radiant sun, the moon — full or as a crescent. The crescent has its positions: horns up by default (montant), turned to dexter (tourné), to sinister (contourné), or down (versé). The star (étoile; five rays in France, six in England) is distinguished from the spur rowel (molette), which resembles it but is pierced at its centre.

Solid round figures are read according to their tincture: in metal, it is a bezant (a memory of the coins brought back from the East by crusaders); in colour, a torteau. A voided round becomes an annulet. These tiny distinctions — metal or colour, solid or pierced — are typical of the stubborn precision of heraldry.

Source: Joubert (1977)
VII.10

The plant kingdom

91 words

Queen of plant charges, the fleur-de-lis — a stylised lily with three petals bound by a band — was the emblem of the kings of France before spreading throughout Europe. The heraldic rose opens with five petals, with its barbs (sepals, often vert) and seeds at the heart. Then come the trefoil, the cinquefoil and the quatrefoil (flowers with five or four petals), the oak and its acorn, the ear of wheat. Each plant bears its symbolism: constancy of the leaf, purity of the lily, strength of the oak.

Source: Joubert (1977)
VII.11

VII.13

The key: pose, groups and meaning

240 words

Among objects, the key is one of the most codified charges: its pose, its direction and the way of grouping two each have a word.

By default, the key stands palewise, the ring in base, the ward (panneton) — the toothed part — in chief, turned to dexter. If the ward looks to sinister, it is contourné. Set bendwise sinister or bendwise, or even fesswise, it normally has the ward turned towards the base: the position in which a key is put into a lock.

In pairs, keys take group names. Addorsed palewise and interlaced (entretenues) when their rings intertwine, as with Clugny: azure, two keys or addorsed palewise, interlaced at the bottom, with lozenge and pommetty rings. In saltire, they cross; if a tie joins their rings, they are bound (liées) — the Comtat Venaissin bears gules, two keys or in saltire, bound azure.

What the key means. It symbolises Saint Peter, whose attribute it is — the key of paradise: a Saint Peter on a shield is recognised by his key (at Eguisheim, he holds it upside down, sable). Two keys in saltire are those of the papal arms, one or, the other argent, also found as an external ornament of the shield. They were often granted by papal favour to arms that aren't those of a pope: the Abbey of Cluny, the House of Clermont-Tonnerre.

The encyclopedia's drawings; the source doesn't say how the wards of a saltire turn, this choice is ours.

Peacock (paon)
In its pride (rouant) when spreading its tail facing out; miraillé if its eyespots differ.
Pelican (pélican)
In its nest, piercing its breast to feed its young: its “piety”.
Partridge (perdrix)
Rather plump, with a short beak.
Eyrie (aire)
The nest of certain birds — pelican, swan, eagle.
Pair of wings (vol)
A pair of wings joined at the joint; a single wing is a demi-vol.

Chimeras

Alphyn
A composite creature close to the griffin, almost solely in English arms.
Amphisbaena (amphisbène)
A dragon whose coiled tail ends in a second serpent's head.
Amphiptere (amphistère)
A winged serpent.
Centaur (centaure)
Armed with a club by default; the centaur-sagittary shoots a bow.
Cerberus (cerbère)
A three-headed dog, guardian of the underworld.
Cerastes (céraste)
A horned serpent.
Chimera (chimère)
A woman's torso, eagle's talons for arms, a goat's body, a serpent's tail.
Bisse
A serpent whose body forms several coils.
Harpy (harpie)
A composite figure belonging to the eagle family.
Hydra (hydre)
A many-headed serpent.
Argus
A flesh-coloured head covered with eyes — the man nothing can deceive.

Plants

Tree (arbre)
Bushy and showing its roots; arraché, terrassé, futé, étagé, écoté or tronqué.
Oak (chêne)
Leaves and acorns drawn one by one; englanté if the acorns differ.
Créquier
A stylised tree with seven branches ending in pike heads, on a few roots.
Fir (sapin)
Symmetrical branches, the ends curving towards the chief.
Willow (saule)
A broad trunk and many thin, leafy branches.
Écot
A tree trunk with its branches cut off.
Chicot
A knotty stick, to be distinguished from the écot, which shows a trunk.
Angemme
An imaginary flower of four to ten petals, pierced at the centre, without stem or leaves.
Poplar leaf (panelle)
A poplar leaf taken singly.
Coquerelle
Three hazelnuts joined by their husks, one towards the chief.
Pomegranate (grenade)
An open fruit showing its seeds, the stalk downwards.
Garden lily (lys)
The garden lily, quite distinct from the heraldic fleur-de-lis.
Vine (vigne, pampre)
A leafy shoot bearing bunches of fruit; the vine stock is shown with its stake.

Heavenly bodies, elements, terrain

Rainbow (arc-en-ciel)
Fesswise or bendwise, barruly or, gules, vert and argent, issuing from the edges.
Aquilon
A chubby child's head blowing the north wind, the blasts in the same tincture.
Flame (flamme)
Rounded at the bottom, ending in wavy tongues; most often gules.
Thunderbolt (foudre)
Masculine in French heraldry: two opposed bundles of flames and darts in saltire.
Moon (lune)
Rarely full; crescent or waning, shown with a human profile.
Sun (soleil)
Figuré when it has a face; otherwise non figuré, éteint, éclipsé, levant or couchant.
Ray (rai)
A ray of the sun, point of a star or rowel, spoke of a wheel, point of an antique crown.
Sea (mer)
Always issuing from the base, occupying the lower third of the field.
Onde
A debated term, held to be a synonym of river and to be used with caution.
Source
Each of the bars wavy crossing a heraldic fountain.
Mount, mountain (mont, montagne)
Plain by default, but most often in hillocks (coupeaux) whose number is blazoned.
Coupeau
One of the hillocks of a mount, rounded like a sugar loaf; never used alone.
Terrace (terrasse)
Ground issuing from the base; isolée if it doesn't touch the edge.
Tertre
A terrace of reduced size.

Weapons and tools

Crossbow (arbalète)
Set palewise, the stock downwards; it shoots bolts (carreaux).
Bow (arc)
Palewise, the string to dexter; couché when fesswise, stringed if the string differs.
Arrow (flèche)
Armed by its head, flighted by its feathers, fûtée by its shaft; versée, encochée.
Badelaire
A scimitar with a short, broad blade, curved towards the point.
Glaive
A synonym of sword; the sword is palewise, point up, dégarnie without its guard.
Lance
A staff widened at the base to protect the hand; brisée, émoussée, fûtée, armée.
Pike (pique)
A polearm with a cylindrical staff, which distinguishes it from the lance.
Otelle
The end of a pike head, also called a peeled almond; four in saltire make the cross of Comminges.
Chape (bouterolle)
The metal fitting at the end of a sword's scabbard.
Cannon (canon)
A piece of artillery, its carriage in another tincture; couleuvrine in the Middle Ages.
War grenade (grenade de guerre)
A spherical bomb with a fuse, lit gules; éclatante when exploding.
Scaling ladder (échelle d'assaut)
A pole fitted with rungs and ending in a hook.
Scales (balance)
Always with two pans.
Caduceus (caducée)
A winged staff entwined by two serpents; a symbol of medicine.
Scissors (ciseaux)
Normally open in saltire; a mainly burgher and craftsman's charge.
Shears (forces)
A pair of blades linked by a spring, for shearing; renversées point down.
Doloire
A hatchet with a short handle and a half-moon blade, the handle set bendwise.
Sickle (faucille)
Shown blade up, pointing to dexter.
Scythe (faux)
Shown like the sickle; a late tool, appearing in the 16th century.
Anille
Two addorsed C curves, linked by crossbars; often confused with the millrind.
Broyes
A wooden knife for breaking the stems of hemp and flax, often in pairs.
Morailles
Pincers for controlling horses by the nostrils; confused with the broyes.
Harrow (herse)
For ploughing, a truncated cone; sarrasine for the portcullis of a gate.
Hie
A tool for ramming paving stones, like a long fusil with two annulets; rare.
Couple
A wooden handle with two cords, used to tie hunting dogs in pairs.
Pilgrim's staff (bourdon)
A pilgrim's staff with a knob; the insignia of dignitaries with no right to the crozier.

Buildings and ships

Building (bâtiment)
Blazoned ajouré, ouvert, maçonné, couvert, essoré, girouetté, hersé or coulissé.
Castle (château)
A fortress with several towers; donjonné when turrets surmount it.
Keep (donjon)
A tower itself bearing turrets.
Avant-mur
A section of wall flanking a tower, issuing from a flank of the shield.
Bridge (pont)
Vaulted, with one or more arches; gardé when turrets occupy its ends.
Aqueduct (aqueduc)
A bridge with many arches, subject to the same rules as the bridge.
Column (colonne)
A Greek column of the Ionic order, in a simplified line.
Ship (navire)
Hull, mast and sail; rigged (équipé, habillé), the sails filled or furled.
Nef
A rigged and dressed ship, as in the arms of Paris.
Galley (galère)
An ancient ship with sails always furled, much used in English arms.

Objects

Ball (boule)
A shaded disc, which distinguishes it from the bezant and torteau, both flat.
Purse (bourse)
A small drawstring bag; liée when the cord is a different tincture.
Firesteel (briquet)
A piece for striking flint; that of Constantinople is confused with a capital B.
Chalice (calice)
A eucharistic cup, often surmounted by a host.
Carreau
A square set on one side; tufted at the corners, it is better blazoned as a cushion.
Channe
A pot with three feet and one handle, also called a marmite à cornière.
Fire pot (pot à feu)
A three-legged pot, without a handle.
Vair bell (cloche de vair)
The azure part of the fur, flared at the bottom; the rest is the argent pot.
Key (clef)
Palewise, the ring in base and the ward in chief turned to dexter; interlaced, bound.
Collar (collier)
A charge in its own right, the external ornament of an order, or an attribute of a collared animal.
Huchet
A small hunting horn without a strap — that is what distinguishes it from the horn.
Grêlier, oliphant (olifant)
A large hunting horn, the knight's; Roland's oliphant.
Crown (couronne)
Used as a charge, often highly stylised; a fragment set bendwise is a crancelin.
Crancelin
A fragment of a crown with fleurons set bendwise, from the arms of Saxony.
Cyclamor
A single ring set on the shield; an obsolete term.
Escutcheon (écusson)
A small shield set on the field; overall when laid at the heart of a quarterly.
Torch (flambeau)
A staff topped by a flame; allumé when the flame is a different tincture.
Fountain (fontaine)
Water flowing in basins; jaillissante if the water is a different tincture.
Love knot (lacs d'amour)
A knot of cord whose shape varies so much that it is better to name the knot.
Book (livre)
Open or closed, sometimes charged with letters such as alpha and omega.
Orb (monde, globe)
A ball banded and topped with a cross — universal sovereignty.
Gibbet (potence)
A gallows; thought too grim, it was sometimes replaced by a tau.
Wheel (roue)
The wheel of Mainz with six spokes, a mill wheel, or a paddle wheel.
Sceptre (sceptre)
An attribute of sovereignty; the diadem is its counterpart for the head.
Barrel (tonneau)
Cerclé when its hoops are a different tincture.
Water bouget (chantepleure)
A double leather bag for carrying water; also called bouse, bouge or bougette.

Bodies and figures

Angel, archangel (ange, archange)
Shown as a young girl wearing a dalmatic, the wings almost always lowered.
Cherub (angelot)
An angel with a child's features; the chérubin is only a head surrounded by wings.
Cupid (amour)
Cupid as a winged child, with a bow and quiver.
Arm (bras)
Dexter or sinister arm; bare by default, clothed or armoured.
Bust (buste)
Head, neck and chest set facing out, without arms.
Clasped hands (foi)
Two right hands clasping, fesswise by default; allegiance.
Hand (main)
Appaumée when showing its palm, contre-appaumée otherwise, blessing with two fingers (bénissante).
Moor's head (tête de Maure)
In profile, in principle sable, wreathed with a band.
Knight (chevalier)
Necessarily in armour and bearing a shield; on horseback, he is the Lithuanian pogonia.
Hercules (Hercule)
Bare-chested with a lion-skin loincloth; his columns mark the Strait of Gibraltar.
Saints
Recognisable by their nimbus and attributes — the key for Peter, the sword for Michael.
Triskelion (triquètre)
Three bent legs joined at the hip in the centre; the Isle of Man, Sicily.
Protomé
A rare term for the head and neck, reserved for the stag.

Figures unique to heraldry

Essonier
An old name for the double tressure.
Fil
The bar of the label; by misuse, the label itself.
Point (pendant)
The tongues of the label, also called gouttes or denticules.
Escarbuncle (escarboucle)
A stone casting eight rays, flory by default; pommetée, fleuronnée, allumée, percée.
Gutty (goutté)
A field semé of drops; drops can be of any tincture, tears are argent.
Ermine spot (moucheture)
Blazoned in a single word: the only spots are ermine ones. Never “ermine” alone.
Vivré
The adjective for the vivre, a sharp zigzag line.
Pièce
A catch-all word in French blazon: donjonné de trois pièces avoids repeating donjons.
Flanquis
Another name for the flanchis, the small couped saltire.
Seal of Solomon (sceau de Salomon)
A six-pointed star, also called a didelta.

A charge of English origin: a bezant filled with azure and crossed by springs argent.

State of knowledge

What is drawn here: the geometric charges, traced from scratch in SVG. They are the ones that best suit vector drawing, and also the ones that can't be seen anywhere else.

What is borrowed: everything with a credit under the figure. Beasts and chimeras first, but also, through successive revisions, the charges whose character our drawing didn't capture — the fleur-de-lis, the martlet, the horn, the cross moline, the ear of wheat, the nettle leaf and a few others. The rule we set ourselves is simple: two attempts, and if the figure still can't be read, we cite a good one, under a free licence, credited beneath it. The credit under the image is authoritative, not this list.

What remains in words: the directory. A hundred and seventy-nine entries without a figure, because an approximate drawing would inform less than an accurate sentence.

Reservation: nobody has closed the list of charges. This one follows the classification and definitions of the French reference list, reworded; regional variants and local usage go far beyond it.

VIII.1

An unchanging reading order

154 words

To blazon is to describe a coat of arms in the technical language of heraldry. The description always follows the same order: first the field (the background) with its tincture; then the main ordinary or charge with its tincture; finally the secondary figures. In French, the tincture comes after the name of the figure; English blazon, built on the same order, puts it after the figure too, but states the field first without a preposition.

  • Azure, three fleurs-de-lis or (d'azur à trois fleurs de lis d'or) — a field azure, charged with three fleurs-de-lis or (the arms of ancient France).
  • Argent, a cross gules (d'argent à la croix de gueules) — a field argent, a red cross (Saint George, Genoa, England).

Done well, a blazon is unambiguous: two artists who have never spoken will produce the same arms from the same sentence. It is a formal language before its time.

Source: Joubert (1977)
VIII.2

Blazoning arms, describing a drawing: two levels of precision

282 words

The level of detail differs depending on whether one wants to blazon arms or describe a particular depiction of those arms.

To blazon arms is to list the significant details that distinguish them from those of another bearer. One stays as general as the heraldic spirit allows, so that precedence can be invoked against all “almost” identical arms, which would differ only in doubtful details. What is secondary or a matter of style is therefore set aside: an unclear animal attitude, an arrangement of charges that may pass for a default position. The fear is that a third party might adopt “almost” the same arms, at the risk of confusion.

Describing a precise depiction — a drawing, a seal, a monument — allows, on the contrary, far greater precision: one can point out apparent irregularities, the solutions chosen, the alternatives set aside. The fear then is the client whose arms were not drawn to their taste.

A lion with no hair on its legs, the tail outside or inside, the head turned right or left, the paw raised or not: the heraldist composing arms readily follows the style of a region or the bearer's wish, but whoever describes the arms must, according to Wikipedia, exercise firm censorship and keep only what belongs to their essential nature. In doubt, it is better to mention the peculiarity when describing the drawing — but to refuse to see in it a sufficient sign to tell these arms from a neighbour's.

This site's Atelier writes blazons of the first kind: only the tincture, the ordinary, the charge, its number and its direction count; the size and position settings given to a charge don't change the text.

Source: Wikipedia (2026)
VIII.3

Composing arms well: easy to say, easy to draw

308 words

A coat of arms is well composed when it is both easy to blazon and easy to draw. Wikipedia thus sums up criteria that the French Commission nationale d'héraldique gathered from various authors; devised for municipalities, they apply almost everywhere. They give no clear-cut verdict, but show where a composition falls short and how to improve it — as long as history hasn't fixed it.

It can fall short in two ways.

  • A correct description, an illegible drawing. A shield de vair treillissé d'hermine et clouté du champ is perfectly understandable; it is impossible to depict correctly, so small do the details become.
  • A drawing that can't be described. Many “modern arms” of countries or municipalities incorporate images, even highly stylised ones, that follow the logic not of heraldry but of the advertising leaflet.

The arms of Liège, which the article considers typical of a late and poorly composed heraldry, serve as an example. They can be blazoned without too much trouble — “gules, a perron of three steps, raised and supported by three lions couchant, bearing a column of three drums, topped by a pine cone and surmounted by a small cross, all or, between a capital L and G of the same” — but they fall short in two ways. The central column calls for a dozen details (lions, pine cone, steps…) that all vanish at small scale: this is no longer an abstract, symbolic figure, but the portrait of a monument, the perron of Liège. And letters are rare in arms, because they aren't universally legible — a heraldist trained in the Cyrillic alphabet would read nothing in them — even if this marginal practice has long been tolerated.

More generally, good composition doesn't stray too far from common usage: it avoids, for example, writing mottoes on the shield itself or composing overly rich pictures.

Source: Wikipedia (2026)
VIII.4

From the field outwards: the order of a complete blazon

300 words

In its strict sense, the blazon describes the shield; in its broad sense, it describes the whole coat of arms. The shield, the only bearer of the elements of identity proper (part of a name, a territory), is always described first, and the text sometimes stops there — for lack of ornaments, or because they add nothing significant.

For simple arms, the field (simple or composite) is stated, then the charges, starting, if there are several, with the main one — the most central, largest or most significant — with everything that charges it; then come the secondary charges accompanying it on the field. For composite arms, each quarter behaves like simple arms. In all cases, the charges or figures affecting the whole are blazoned last.

External ornaments — crowns, collars, supporters and terrace, mantles, flags, motto… — come next, from the foreground back and from the shield outwards; but this order isn't strictly fixed, and varies by place and period. The language there is less rigorous than within the shield, and shortcuts are acceptable: for flags, it is clearer to write “of France, Germany and Poland” than to describe them one by one.

A simple example, ancient Alsace: per pale, first, gules, a bend sinister argent between two bendlets fleuronny of the same, and second, also gules, a bend or between six crowns of the same, three in chief and three inverted in base. One reads first the division (“per pale”), then, for each part, a field, an ordinary, and the figures around it.

In principle, given arms should correspond to a single text: for simple arms, practice confirms this; for complex arms, synonymous variants are common enough. Try this order in the Atelier: it always writes the field, then the ordinary, then the charges.

Source: Wikipedia (2026) · Joubert (1977)
VIII.5

More than a hundred and fifty words: the arms of the Dukes of Mayenne

194 words

Wikipedia cites, as a complex example, the arms of the Dukes of Mayenne, which are blazoned in a single sentence of more than a hundred and fifty French words. Read from afar, the sentence is a nesting: quarterly 1 and 4 and 2 and 3; the grand quarter of 1 and 4 is itself divided into eight parts, listed by the text (“first… eighth”); an escutcheon overall — or, a bend gules charged with three alerions argent — is differenced by a label gules; the grand quarter of 2 and 3 is quarterly again, with notably three fleurs-de-lis or within a bordure indented gules and or. Each part reads like simple arms; the art is not to lose the thread.

The same page reads these arms as those of a couple: they would be, in the genealogy of the Dukes of Guise, those of Francis of Guise, known as le Balafré (1519-1563), and Anne of Este and Ferrara. From this union came a younger son, Charles of Mayenne, ancestor of the cadet branch — and the label gules differencing the escutcheon is the usual mark of a cadet branch (see the chapter “Differencing”).

Source: Wikipedia (2026)
VIII.6

The text is free, the drawing has an author

126 words

In France, according to the article cited, the blazon itself — even one written today — isn't protected by copyright: it is a description. The drawing of a coat of arms, on the other hand, whether or not it starts from a blazon, counts as a graphic work bearing “the personal imprint of its author”; it is protected, by the mere fact of its creation, until seventy years after the author's death.

This is the division this site respects: blazons are facts that can be cited, but drawings have authors — its own, published under a free licence, and those of Wikimedia Commons, credited one by one (see the page “Real arms”). This isn't legal advice: it reports what the article says, for French law only.

Source: Wikipedia (2026)

IX.1

Around the shield

110 words

The shield can be ensigned (timbré) and accompanied by ornaments which, for their part, aren't passed on like the arms but mark rank and office:

  • the helm (heaume), whose position and metal formerly indicated status;
  • the crest (cimier), a figure set on top of the helm;
  • the mantling (lambrequins), cut foliage framing the shield, a memory of the veil protecting the neck;
  • the crown or hat, signs of title or of ecclesiastical dignity;
  • the supporters (tenants and supports), figures holding up the shield;
  • the motto (devise) and the war cry (cri), words written on a scroll.

IX.2

The hierarchy of coronets of rank

588 words

Set on the helm — or, once the helmet fell out of use, directly on top of the shield — the crown is the ornament that most quickly states the bearer's rank. The system took shape fairly late, mainly in the 16th-17th centuries, then was taken up and codified by 19th-century heraldists. There has never been a single official standard: sources vary, sometimes appreciably, on the exact number of pearls or fleurons given to each rank — a sign that this is less a matter of law than of usage.

The series most often cited for the French nobility, from top to bottom:

  • Royal crown — a gold circle topped with eight fleurs-de-lis, closed by pearled diadems meeting at the top in a double fleur-de-lis: a closed crown, the sovereign's sole privilege.
  • Ducal coronet — a gold circle raised with eight fleurons, without pearls, left open.
  • Marquess's coronet — a circle decorated with four fleurons alternating with groups of pearls (sources speak sometimes of “trefoils” of three pearls, sometimes of single pearls).
  • Count's coronet — no more fleurons at all: a circle raised with pearls alone, whose number varies by author (eight pearls in a row for some, sixteen pearls with nine visible for others).
  • Viscount's coronet — a small number of large pearls, usually four, only three of which appear from the front according to some authors.
  • Baron's coronet — a plain circle, without points, surrounded by a row or string of small pearls; some authors give it only a wreath, with no coronet of its own.
  • Knight and simple gentleman — no coronet: the helm bears as an ornament of rank only a wreath or torse (bourrelet, torque), a twisted ribbon in the colours of the shield.

The comparison with the British peerage shows that these grammars are national rather than universal: among the English, the duke bears eight strawberry leaves, the marquess four leaves and four pearls, the earl eight leaves and eight pearls on stems, the viscount sixteen pearls, the baron six — a similar principle (the higher the rank, the more fleurons outweigh pearls) but a different detail.

Two crowns fall outside this noble scale: the mural crown, with crenellated towers, reserved for the arms of towns and municipalities, and the papal tiara, a triple crown topped by a cross on a globe, unique to the pope.

The principle is easier to see than to describe: a plain shield gules, with no other indication, reads as the arms of a simple gentleman; the same shield, ensigned with a gold circle with fleurons, at once becomes a ducal coat of arms. The field doesn't change — only the crown speaks of rank. The full scale is given below, from the knight's wreath to the sovereign's closed crown, with the mural crown of towns and the papal tiara set apart, both off the noble scale. Seven of these nine figures come from a single series, which allows them to be compared in the same style.

IX.3

The hierarchy of fiefs: from duchy to lordship

426 words

The title borne (duke, count, baron…) and the fief that went with it don't always coincide: by the end of the Ancien Régime, a title could remain purely honorific, detached from any real land. There was, however, on paper, a hierarchy of the fiefs themselves, indexed to their composition: a treatise of 1764 requires the duchy and marquisate to bring together at least three baronies and three castellanies, and the barony at least three castellanies. Going down the scale:

  • Duchy — the largest; the only title to enjoy, under the Ancien Régime, a truly recognised precedence. The duché-pairie also gave a rank and a seat in the Parlement.
  • Marquisate — theoretically of the same territorial extent as the duchy, but of lesser precedence.
  • County
  • Viscounty — originally the jurisdiction of a viscount, the count's deputy.
  • Barony
  • Lordship (seigneurie) — the commonest level, with no courtesy title attached.
  • Castellany (châtellenie) — the basic district, added together to form the upper levels.

This scale should be taken with caution: apart from the rank of duke, there was no official, binding hierarchy of titles under the Ancien Régime — the antiquity of the house and its alliances counted at least as much as the title borne. The system only became rigid from 1808, under a new logic: that of the nobility of the Empire and then the Restoration, where the baron rewards a senior official, the count a minister, the duke and prince feats of arms — a nobility of office, no longer of land.

The clearest example of this hierarchy in action remains the peerage of France: at its origin, in the 12th century, it gathers twelve great fiefs giving a seat and a voice at the king's coronation — six lay (the Duchies of Normandy, Burgundy and Aquitaine, the Counties of Toulouse, Flanders and Champagne) and six ecclesiastical (the Archbishopric of Reims and the Bishoprics of Langres, Beauvais, Châlons, Noyon and Laon). The list shifts over time — the Duchy of Normandy, joined early to the crown, is replaced by other duchés-pairies — but it shows clearly that rank (peer) and fief (duchy or county) were, originally, one and the same thing.

Not all fiefs bear such a high courtesy title: the Viscounty of Béarn, for example, remains a case debated by historians, its viscounts having ended up behaving as de facto sovereigns without ever formally depending on an overlord count — a sign that the duchy/county/viscounty/barony grid describes a usage more than a rigid law, with its borderline cases.

Source: Wikipedia (2026)
IX.4

Common ornaments and specific ornaments

458 words

Not all the ornaments around the shield are equal: some are, in principle, available to anyone bearing arms; others are reserved — by tradition more than by law, in France — for a specific rank, office or dignity.

Common ornaments, open to any armiger:

  • the helm, of which only the metal and the opening of the visor remain an indication of rank (gold and wide open for the sovereign, silver or steel with a more closed grille going down the scale);
  • the crest, set on top of the helmet;
  • the mantling, which falls from it in cut foliage, a memory of the cloth protecting the combatant's neck;
  • the wreath or torse, which takes the place of a coronet for those with no right to one;
  • the motto, a brief sentence written on a scroll beneath the shield (the “Preny! Preny!” of the Dukes of Lorraine), and the older war cry, shouted in combat and placed above instead.

Specific ornaments, reserved for a rank or office:

  • coronets of rank (see the previous article), from the mural crown of towns to the sovereign's closed crown;
  • the mantle, a drapery usually lined with ermine (with vair for the Dukes of the Empire) that envelops the shield — the prerogative of sovereigns, princes and dukes; the peers of France bore a particular azure mantle;
  • the pavilion, which tops the mantle and sets it further apart: reserved for the sovereign alone, it marks the summit of the hierarchy;
  • the supporters — human figures (tenants) and animals (supports) — flanking the shield: more freely used in France than in certain foreign traditions, where their use is strictly regulated by heraldic law;
  • the ecclesiastical hat or galero, whose colour and number of tassels state dignity in the Church: red with thirty tassels (fifteen on each side) for a cardinal, green with twenty for an archbishop, green with twelve for a bishop — a number in which some have seen an echo of the apostles — then black, with fewer and fewer tassels, for lower dignities (abbot, prior, protonotary); the general rule fits in one sentence: the higher the dignity, the more tassels, though the sources consulted give no fixed count below the episcopal rank;
  • the collars of orders of chivalry (Holy Spirit, Golden Fleece, Legion of Honour…), set around the shield and reserved for actual members of the order.

The distinction remains one of usage more than of regulation: apart from orders of chivalry and, to a lesser extent, coronets, nothing legally prevented anyone in France from adorning their arms with supporters or a mantle without holding the rank — hence the caution with which old armorials are read today.

Source: Wikipedia (2026) · Aleteia (2023)
IX.5

Above and below the shield: crest, war cry, motto

272 words

The crest is the ornament that tops a helmet or helm. Its first purpose is military: it enlarges the bearer's silhouette to impress the opponent, and serves as a landmark on the battlefield. In the Middle Ages, it is a parade ornament, meant to strike spectators before entering the tournament, not to withstand blows. When the helm is part of the shield's external ornaments, the crest often repeats a figure from the arms; a helmet with a pointed top can bear a plume, a flat-topped one more elaborate decorations — heads and necks of animals, issuant busts or limbs, wings, horns, small banners.

Words share the space around the shield. The war cry — a word or phrase common to the members of an army or company, used to recognise and motivate one another before combat — appears above the arms; the motto — a short phrase suggesting an ideal, a rule of conduct or a glorious past — goes below. Both are written on a scroll (listel), a ribbon or band usually set beneath the shield, but also above, or all around like a collar.

Some well-known mottoes: “Dieu et mon droit”, that of the sovereign of the United Kingdom; “Honi soit qui mal y pense”, that of the Order of the Garter; “Fluctuat nec mergitur”, that of the city of Paris; “Je maintiendrai”, that of the Netherlands; “Je me souviens”, that of Quebec. As for cries: “Montjoie! Saint Denis!” for France, “Moult me tarde” for Philip the Bold, “Ainsi je frappe” for Charles the Bold. In the Atelier, the motto sits on a band under the shield.

Chapter X

The law of arms

Heraldry isn't only an art: it is also, at times, a matter of law. Three texts changed not the taste for arms, but the way they had to be drawn.

X.1

Who may bear arms?

240 words

This is the most stubborn misconception: that coats of arms are a privilege of the nobility. They never were. In France, the use of arms remained free and without charge throughout the Ancien Régime: townspeople, craftsmen, trade communities, parishes and guilds commonly bore them. Canon law says the same with a clarity one wouldn't expect from Rome — the right to bear any signs is held there to be “a natural right of every human personality”, which “is not limited to privileged classes”.

In essence, there is only one truly universal rule of law: one doesn't take another's arms. Everything else is free. The edict of 1696 says nothing else: its article 12 forbids usurping another's arms — which implies that, with that reservation, everyone is free to take whichever they wish.

The customary corollary of this rule is called the right of precedence: in a dispute, whoever has borne the arms longest prevails. Formed under the Ancien Régime, it still holds today. To settle these disputes, Louis XIII had created in 1615 the office of Judge General of Arms of France (Juge général d'Armes), held by the d'Hozier family.

One last detail, a warning against a whole genealogical literature: the certificates of arms issued in 1696 themselves stated, in article 17, that they could “in no case be taken as proof of nobility”. Having arms never proved one was noble. It proved one had paid.

X.2

1696 — the edict that taxed coats of arms

616 words

In November 1696, the treasury of Louis XIV was drained: the War of the League of Augsburg had lasted seven years. The Controller-General Pontchartrain, who had just invented the capitation, proposed to the king a census of all the arms in the kingdom — and to charge for registration. The edict of November 1696 created the Armorial général de France.

On paper, the measure was twofold: at last to provide a public register making the ownership of arms enforceable, and to fill the coffers. It established a “general mastership of arms” of thirty-seven officers and twenty-eight provincial masterships. Almost nobody bought these offices: they were abolished by August 1700. It was Charles René d'Hozier, appointed keeper of the Armorial by an order of the Council of 18 December 1696, who ran the enterprise alone.

The fee was twenty livres per person — a pittance for a noble, a sum for a craftsman. And the constraint was real: two months after closure, using unregistered arms meant a fine of 300 livres, a third of it for the informer, and confiscation of furniture, silverware and armorial carriages. In the wake of this, an order of 22 July 1698 made “fleurs-de-lis or on a field azure” subject to authorisation: bearers who couldn't justify them saw their lilies replaced by bezants, chess rooks or stars. These files form the registers known as the armoiries sursises (suspended arms).

Arms by decree

Refusal was massive. Court nobles considered themselves above the formality, monks considered themselves civilly dead, townspeople refused out of avarice or defiance; in Auvergne and Béarn, opposition reached 80%. The king then authorised the intendants to draw up tax rolls (3 December 1697), then d'Hozier to assign arms automatically to those who paid without declaring anything (22 May 1699).

The clerks at first composed canting arms, that is, the name turned into a rebus: Revellois received a cock waking (réveillant) four geese, Pinguet a pine (pin) and a jay (geai), Clarentin a bell and some thyme, Bouache a billy goat (bouc) and two axes (haches). Then, for two years, they amused themselves at the expense of the recalcitrant. Sieur Malaunay was saddled with “a man's face with a pimple on the nose”; the lawyers of Quimper received “a saddled donkey argent”, the bailiffs of Nantes “a devil”, an apothecary a syringe and three chamber pots, the parish priest Raudot “a bomb sable”, the Plotton family of Lyon “a hedgehog gules” — that is, bloodied.

1701: the state mass-produces coats of arms

The joke ended with the order of 16 September 1701, which set up a system of mechanical composition of arms. The principle was purely combinatorial: two metals (or, argent), each combined with four tinctures (azure, gules, vert, sable), give eight combinations; field and ordinary are then swapped, completing a series of sixteen. Indented was exhausted over fifteen series, then engrailed, then invected, varying the ordinary — fess, bend, bend sinister, chevron — and then their number. In 1703 the system grew more complex: the ordinary was charged with a charge, and each charge then offered forty-eight possibilities instead of sixteen. In the généralité of Lyon, all the recalcitrant received a chevron charged with a charge: sixteen charges, forty-eight combinations each, 768 possible coats of arms — 725 were actually used.

The Armorial quietly closed around June 1710. It contains 125,807 painted coats of arms. Nearly half — about 48% — had never been borne by anybody: they were invented by the administration to collect a tax. It is the largest armorial in Europe, and the reason it must be used with caution.

X.3

1808 — rank enters the shield

301 words

By the decrees of 1 March 1808, Napoleon created the titles of the Empire and at once gave them heraldic regulations. The reversal was complete, and it is the only case in which a French law changed the very drawing of arms.

Until 1789, rank was read around the shield: crown, helm, mantle, supporters. From 1808, it was read inside. Crowns were abolished for everyone except the imperial family and sovereign princes; in their place came a black velvet toque with a plume, differing for a grand dignitary, duke, count, baron or knight. And the title itself became an ordinary on the shield:

  • Princes — a chief azure semé of bees or, the bee being the dynastic emblem;
  • Dukes — a chief gules semé of stars argent;
  • Counts — a quarter azure;
  • Barons — a quarter gules.

This quarter doesn't state rank alone: it is itself charged with a mark of office. Soldiers bear a sword argent hilted or, senators a serpent argent encircling a mirror or. Marshals add behind the shield two batons azure semé of eagles or, in saltire. A heraldry of office, where the Ancien Régime had a heraldry of birth.

A word on vocabulary: the term franc-quartier (quarter) is used by convention, but it is improper, since the ordinary doesn't occupy a quarter of the shield — its dimensions adapt to the figures so as not to hinder reading.

These rules must be seen for what they are: heraldic law, not grammar. They say who must bear what according to rank; they scarcely touch the rules of blazon themselves. And they didn't outlive their regime: on the morrow of Waterloo, most families simply had the imperial quarter erased, and only a few starred chiefs, azure quarters and gules cantons remain in arms.

X.4

1969 — Rome forbids the mitre and crozier

582 words

The Church has its own heraldic law, and it rests on a principle opposite to that of the nobility: the right to ornaments is founded not on birth, but on office and consecration. A peasant's son who becomes a bishop has a right to all that a bishop has a right to. The logical consequence: these insignia are neither hereditary nor transferable.

A second principle, decisive for understanding 1969: Rome never legislates on the shield. What the field contains belongs to the personal right of the cleric, acquired before entering the ecclesiastical state. The object of the Church's heraldic law is exclusively “all that is added outside the shield”.

Hence a long series of texts that, century after century, remove ornaments:

  • 20 November 1611 — an ordinance of the Congregation of Bishops and Regulars forbids chapters from arbitrarily adorning their arms with the mitre and crozier;
  • 16 December 1644 — Militantis Ecclesiæ Regimini, of Innocent X;
  • 15 January 1915 — a decree of the Consistorial Congregation forbids patriarchs, archbishops and bishops from showing in their seals and arms noble titles, crowns and secular marks, unless a secular dignity is attached to the see, and apart from the orders of Malta and the Holy Sepulchre;
  • 12 May 1951 — under Pius XII, a new decree removes even this exception: no more noble coronets on episcopal arms;
  • 31 March 1969 — the instruction Ut sive sollicite;
  • 9 November 1989 — the decree Domus Ecclesiae extends the use of the pavilion and keys to minor basilicas.

What the 1969 instruction does

Signed by Cardinal Amleto Cicognani for the Secretariat of State and published in the Acta Apostolicae Sedis (vol. 61, p. 334), the instruction Ut sive sollicite of 31 March 1969 forbids on episcopal arms two figures that had been there since the 13th century: the mitre and the crozier. Yet they were the very first pontifical insignia that heraldic art had adopted as marks of dignity.

What remains, and is now enough to read an ecclesiastical coat of arms:

  • the processional cross set palewise behind the shield — one crossbar for a bishop, two for an archbishop, primate and patriarch. It is the only sign that bishops alone have the right to bear;
  • the hat (galero) and its cord, whose colour marks the nature of the office and whose number of tassels grows with dignity;
  • for metropolitan archbishops, the pallium, a band of white wool charged with black crosses, usually placed at the base of the shield.

A point that matters

It is readily said that “Vatican II changed heraldry”. This is true of the spirit, false of the letter: Ut sive sollicite isn't a conciliar document. The Council closed in 1965; the heraldic text is a post-conciliar instruction of the Secretariat of State, issued under Paul VI four years later. And like any disciplinary rule, it is unevenly followed: in practice, the mitre still reappears here and there in the arms of bishops or mitred abbots.

The most instructive contrast lies elsewhere. The 1969 instruction binds only Rome. Since the Church of England isn't concerned, its bishops and archbishops have continued to ensign their arms with the mitre — and have developed their own code: sable hats at all ranks, purpure cords for archdeacons and deacons, sable for canons, argent for priests; six tassels for an archdeacon, two for a priest. Two Churches, one heraldic tradition, separated by an administrative text of 1969.

XI.1

Scotland — a court, a prosecutor, a register

370 words

Nowhere is heraldry more legal than in Scotland. In 1592, the Parliament of the kingdom found that many subjects were assuming arms that didn't belong to them, to the point that gentlemen by birth could no longer be told by their arms. It gave the Lord Lyon King of Arms and his heralds the power to visit all the arms of the kingdom, to distinguish them with suitable differences, to matriculate them in their registers — and to forbid common people whom “the law of arms” doesn't deem worthy from bearing them. The sanction was material: confiscation, for the king, of goods on which the arms were engraved or painted, and a fine of a hundred pounds for the Lyon.

In 1672, a second act found that the “irregularities of these late times” had multiplied arms taken without right, and ratified the first. Nobles, barons and gentlemen had to declare the arms they bore within the year, with supporting documents; the Lyon distinguished and matriculated them; and the register thus formed, the Public Register of All Arms and Bearings in Scotland, was declared the “true and unrepealable rule” of all arms in the country. After a year and a day, bearing other arms cost a hundred pounds per offence, with confiscation of the armorial goods.

Both acts are still in force, trimmed at the margins (in 1906, 1987 and 1995). And the court works: a procurator fiscal, appointed by the Scottish ministers, decides whether to prosecute the illegal use of arms before the Lord Lyon. The Lyon exercises two functions of different kinds: the grant of new arms, an administrative act that can only be challenged by judicial review, and matriculation, the re-registration of existing arms, a judicial act that can be appealed to the Court of Session and then the Supreme Court.

The most disconcerting consequence for a French reader: there is no such thing in Scotland as “family arms”. Arms belong to only one person at a time; even the heir must bear them differenced. A clan has no arms either — only its chief does; members wear a badge made of the chief's crest, in a buckled belt bearing his motto.

XI.2

England — the court that no longer sat

255 words

England has its own jurisdiction, the High Court of Chivalry: the court of the Constable and Marshal, which appeared in the 14th century, competent for military matters and, by extension, for the use of arms. Whittled away by the common-law courts, it ceased to meet after 1737. It was thought extinct for two centuries.

On 21 December 1954, it sat again. The city of Manchester charged a theatre, the Manchester Palace of Varieties, with displaying its arms in the auditorium for over twenty years and on its seal for over sixty. The Duke of Norfolk, hereditary Earl Marshal, had delegated his seat to the Lord Chief Justice, Lord Goddard; the hearing was held at the Royal Courts of Justice, the hall of the College of Arms being too small.

The stakes went beyond the theatre: it first had to be known whether the court still existed. The defence argued that a statute of Richard II gave it jurisdiction only over “deeds of arms”, in the warlike sense, and that decorative use wasn't a bearing of arms. Goddard declared the court competent and ordered the theatre to pay the agreed costs, 300 pounds; the city won its case.

The scope of the case was debated from 1955. The Heraldry Society's commentator, J. P. Brooke-Little, saw it mainly as a reminder that there is an enforceable law of arms — while predicting that the bearing of arms would remain governed, in England, by custom and good manners. The court has not met since.

XI.3

Canada — arms as a state distinction

170 words

Until 1988, a Canadian wanting properly granted arms had to apply to London or Edinburgh. By letters patent of 1988, the Queen of Canada authorised the Governor General to exercise “all powers” of the Crown in the matter of granting arms in Canada; the Canadian Heraldic Authority was born on 4 June 1988. Canada thus became the first Commonwealth realm, outside the United Kingdom, to have its own heraldic authority.

The model is neither French nor Scottish: arms there are a distinction conferred in the name of the Crown. Any Canadian citizen or corporate body may apply, and the Chief Herald accepts or rejects the application; one pays the heralds and artists, not for the arms, which are deemed given. The Authority also registers historical emblems and those of First Nations, and approves the badges and flags of the Armed Forces.

Two further features: women there inherit and pass on arms equally with men, and the holder can protect their arms as a trademark, under the Trademarks Act.

XI.4

Austria — abolition, right down to burghers' arms

271 words

At the opposite extreme from Scotland, republican Austria abolished everything. The law of 3 April 1919, passed unanimously by the Constituent National Assembly, abolished the nobility, its honorific prerogatives and a series of titles; it now has constitutional rank. The implementing ordinance of 18 April 1919 lists what disappears, and its § 2 goes further than one might imagine: after the particle von, predicates and titles, it abolishes “the right to bear family arms, notably also those wrongly called ‘burgher’ arms”.

Of the five countries presented here, it is the only one where the right to arms is taken from all, commoners included. The ordinance allows a practical exception: using objects already bearing an abolished mark of nobility isn't punishable.

The sanction, for its part, died out by itself. The law provided for a fine of up to 20,000 crowns or six months' arrest; the crown has disappeared, and the amount, set in a constitutional text, was never converted. Called upon in 2019 about a conviction of Karl Habsburg-Lothringen for the particle shown in his website's address, the Constitutional Court ruled that no fine could be imposed any more; it left open the question of arrest.

There remains a nuance, showing that abolition doesn't erase everything. In 1994, the Austrian Supreme Court heard a case about a hotelier's use of a German's family arms, granted to an ancestor in 1631. It ruled that the existence of these arms fell under German law, the claimant's personal statute, and filled the gap in Austrian law by applying by analogy the protection of the name — before nevertheless dismissing the claim.

Source: Autriche (1919) · Wikipedia (2026)
XI.5

Germany and Switzerland — arms protected like a name

395 words

Germany took a third path. Since the end of the monarchy, in 1918, the state no longer grants, modifies or confirms any family arms. Anyone may adopt arms by simple declaration, without formality. Heraldic societies keep “rolls of arms” in which they can be entered for a fee, which makes it easier to prove precedence; the doctrines these societies have developed have no legal force.

Protection comes from § 12 of the Civil Code (BGB), which allows the holder of a name to demand the end of an unauthorised use and to seek an injunction if the interference may recur. The text says not a word about arms: it is case law that extends it to them by analogy, provided they are distinctive enough to designate someone. The extension is now taken as settled, but a minority opinion still refuses to protect what it calls a “historical fossil”. Notably, according to the same source, the courts of the Federal Republic have never yet had to decide between two families claiming the same arms. The arms of the state and the Länder fall under another regime; their unauthorised use there is an administrative offence.

Switzerland combines the two logics. For private arms, there is no special law: anyone may take them, and they are protected as an attribute of personality, like the name (art. 28 of the Civil Code). For public arms, on the contrary, the text is tight. The law on the protection of coats of arms of 21 June 2013, in force since 1 January 2017, reserves the arms of the Confederation, cantons and municipalities for the community concerned, forbids assigning or licensing them, and punishes their unlawful use — on products, signs, websites — with up to a year's imprisonment or a fine.

Two articles of this law are of direct interest to the heraldist. The first blazons, with a precision no armorial attempts: the Swiss cross is “a white, vertical, couped cross on a red ground”, whose arms, equal to one another, are “a sixth longer than they are wide” — and the annex fixes the red by its exact code, where heraldry knows only gules. The eighth allows these arms to be used “as an illustration in a dictionary, a reference work, a scientific work”: it is on this basis that an encyclopedia like this one can show them.

XII.1

Differing without disowning

102 words

Within a family, the plain arms go to the head of the name and arms; the other members difference them (briser), that is, change them slightly to stand apart without breaking their belonging. The best-known method is the label (lambel), a small bar with points placed in chief, the mark of the eldest son while his father lives.

Systems of differencing vary by country and period: the English system of cadency marks (label, crescent, mullet…) is more rigid than the freer French practice. Handle with care: presenting a local usage as a universal rule is a frequent error.

Source: Joubert (1977)
XII.2

Four ways of differencing

255 words

To difference is to slightly change inherited arms so they aren't confused with those of the head of the line. Wikipedia notes four common methods: diminishing an honourable ordinary; changing the tincture of an important element, the field or an ordinary; adding a lesser ordinary or a charge; and, especially in Germany, changing the crest — branches of one family there recognise one another by crests differing in number or shape.

In France, the marks of difference most often met are the bordure, the label and the bâton péri, set bendwise or bendwise sinister (péri: shortened, so that it doesn't reach the edge of the shield). A few conventions exist — the bend passes for a sign of legitimacy, the label for a younger son's difference — but they require considerable historical knowledge of whoever is blazoning: outside the great houses, they are scarcely applied. The bend sinister, readily attributed to younger sons, is best known as a mark of bastardy (see below); Wikipedia's list of ordinaries recalls that taking it as a younger son's difference is far from a general rule.

The word baton itself has no stable definition. According to the same source, authors fall into three families: a bend or bend sinister reduced by half (Crayencourt); a bendlet, that is, a narrowed bend (Pastoureau); a simple synonym of bendlet, used in its place when it is shortened, raguly or flory (Duhoux d'Argicourt). This is one of those points on which it is best not to rule.

XII.3

Reading differenced arms

136 words

The reading rule is simple: the plain arms are blazoned first, as if the difference didn't exist, then one concludes with differenced by…. Wikipedia cites the arms of the Bastard of Orléans: azure, three fleurs-de-lis or, differenced in chief by a label argent and by a bend sinister argent over all.

Common usage is less rigorous. The arms of the French department of Loiret, for example, read “… all surmounted by a label argent”, where the exact wording would be “… differenced by a label argent in chief”. Nor can a birth rank be deduced from a difference without knowing the family: the same mark has changed meaning across times and houses — the eldest son's label becomes that of the younger ones, the bend of bastardy becomes a difference of legitimate younger sons.

XII.4

The label, from eldest to younger son

228 words

The label is a horizontal band fitted with points — usually three; when the number differs, the blazon says so. It is usually placed in chief. The French word, attested in 1165, is a form of lambeau (shred): it evokes the toothed cut-outs that edged certain medieval garments.

Its use has changed with time. Originally, it served as a difference for the eldest son during his father's lifetime, the father being the only one who could personally bear the family's plain arms; on the father's death, the eldest took the plain arms and the label passed to the younger son. Families descended from younger sons then kept the habit of bearing it, making it the most used difference in France. So books say either “mark of the eldest” or “mark of a younger son”: these are two states of usage, not two errors.

Its shape has varied too: a bar across the full width of the shield with rectangular points in the Middle Ages, trapezoidal flared points from the 15th century, a bar detached from the edges in the 16th, a return to medieval forms today. A few labels escape the usual place: that of the Lords of Beaujeu is in the middle of the shield — 17th-century scholars called it “over all” — and modern Holland readily places it at the centre.

XII.5

The English marks of cadency

257 words

England codified what France left free: to each son, according to birth rank, a small figure placed at the centre of the shield — except the label, which stays in chief. In order: the label of three points (first son), the crescent (second), the mullet (third), the martlet (fourth), the annulet (fifth), the fleur-de-lis (sixth), the rose (seventh), then the cross moline (eighth) and the double quatrefoil, also called the octofoil (ninth). The eldest son of the eldest bears a label of five points; grandchildren combine their father's mark with their own, giving piles that quickly become illegible. Daughters have no mark of their own: they bear their father's arms on a lozenge. An illegitimate child bears a bordure wavy.

Yet one must beware of making this a rule: codified doesn't mean compulsory. The College of Arms presents these marks as optional and reports that the system was supposedly invented around 1500 by John Writhe, Garter; Peter Gwynn-Jones, Garter King of Arms, recommends using them only sparingly; Oswald Barron already noted that the practice had long been neglected — stained glass, bookplates and monuments mostly ignore it. Only Scotland enforces it, through the registration of arms with the Lord Lyon (see the chapter “Elsewhere”).

The sources don't say quite the same thing: the French Wikipedia page writes that in England and Scotland the eldest must difference with a label; the English page, more detailed on this point, states that the system is optional in England and only enforced in Scotland. The latter is followed here.

XII.6

Marks of bastardy

320 words

When differencing took hold, in the 13th century, illegitimate sons had no special mark; from the 14th, certain signs were more readily kept for them, though the usage wasn't universal. The historian Laurent Hablot, whom Wikipedia follows, distinguishes three systems.

  • The plain shield (uniform, often argent or or), bearing the father's arms — sometimes the mother's — in an ordinary, usually a quarter. Hablot links it with romances of chivalry, where the plain shield belongs to young knights who must prove themselves. In the 15th century, the quarter disappears and the system turns around: from then on, it is the father's arms, differenced by an ordinary of metal.
  • The “device system”: the bastard takes up emblematic elements of his father's, notably in England (Wikipedia flags this point as needing a source).
  • The bend sinister (barre), which prevailed from the 15th century, sometimes reduced to a bendlet or a thin line. It stands opposite the bend, a common difference for legitimate sons. Letters by which the King of France granted arms from the mid-15th century, and legal texts such as the Customs of Lorraine of 1594, make it the sign of natural children.

The movement has its reversal: signs of bastardy tend to be turned into differences of legitimate younger sons. Charles of Beaumont, a bastard of the royal house of Navarre, received in 1391 arms quartering Navarre and Beaumont in place of his quarter of Navarre. The descendants of Peter of Bourbon-Busset, who bore his father's arms on a pale under a chief of Jerusalem as a sign of bastardy, differenced only with the chief in the 17th century. The House of Orléans-Longueville, descended from Jean de Dunois, Bastard of Orléans and the first Capetian to difference with a bend sinister, displays only a bâton péri set bendwise. In England, the mark of the illegitimate child is a bordure wavy.

XIII.1

Two arms, one shield: per pale, quarterly, dimidiated

334 words

Composite arms are formed of simpler arms, set side by side along a division or assembled in quarters. They are better understood as several shields united in one than as one shield split into several: the blank shield is divided, then each element of the division behaves as a shield in its own right — and can be divided in turn. The elements are of equal size, but don't have the same prestige: it decreases from top to bottom, and from dexter to sinister.

A very frequent use of divisions is to express unions of all kinds: marriages, annexed fiefs… A union of two is often made by a per pale (parti), which squeezes the figures in width and stresses the precedence of dexter — which may be intended — or by a quarterly (écartelé), which doesn't distort the original shield and gives a more equal union: the most and least prestigious quarters for one, the two middle ones for the other.

Dimidiation (mi-parti) is another way of uniting two coats of arms: the dexter half of one is joined to the sinister half of the other — provided the originals remain identifiable once cut in two. The Wikipedia article advises against the French expression coupé mi-parti, which is ambiguous: better to place the per pale in the part of the per fess it concerns.

To blazon composite arms, the division is stated first, then the elementary arms, in the order of the division, preceded by their rank: “per pale, first argent, a torteau gules, second or, a fess azure”. The rule of order is “top before bottom, dexter before sinister”; identical quarters are read together with the first met; and when a quarter is itself divided, the rule applies in cascade (1, 2, 3, 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, 5, 6).

The Atelier can compose and read the quarterly (« aux 1 et 4… ; aux 2 et 3… »), with or without an overall escutcheon.

XIII.2

The arms of a married woman

326 words

For a long time, a married woman using arms displayed the shields of her husband, to dexter, and of her father, to sinister, side by side (accolé); or a shield per pale of her husband's arms to dexter and her father's to sinister. Arms of alliance, when composed of two shields, are placed side by side or leaning towards each other — that the shields sometimes lean outwards is only a detail of composition — and in principle surmounted by the coronet of the husband's title.

Usages vary. In France, arms of alliance are found with two oval shields (a composition made for the wife) or with two shields side by side (a composition made for the husband). In Belgium, the wife's shield is always oval, the husband's “French-style”. On a single shield, the alliance is made by a per pale for the couple, the husband's arms to dexter, the wife's to sinister; or by a quarterly for members of the line descending from the couple, with, where appropriate, a difference according to the bearer's rank.

British heraldry is more codified. Women there generally don't inherit arms or pass them on, except when there is no male heir: a heraldic heiress marrying an armigerous man gives her son the right to quarter. Unmarried, a woman bears her arms on a lozenge or an oval; married, she may bear arms impaling her husband's and her own; widowed, she keeps her marriage arms, but on a lozenge or oval, whereas a widower simply takes up his own again. A woman bears no crest, held to be a male attribute. Since 2014, rules also exist for same-sex marriages.

The lozenge is therefore a container here: the shape of the shield states the sex and status of the bearer, not the composition of the arms (see the chapter “The shield”). The chapter “Differencing” recalls this for the English cadency system: daughters bear their father's arms on a lozenge.

XIII.3

Heraldic courtesy: turning out of politeness

288 words

Heraldic courtesy is the name for changing a coat of arms for the sake of beauty, balance or harmony. The typical example is that of arms of alliance: the husband's lion, which usually looks to dexter, would turn its back on the wife's arms, set to sinister; out of courtesy, it is turned (contourné) to look towards them — and, in fact, the whole coat of arms is reversed. The same gesture applies to an eagle, and to the husband's helm, turned so as not to show its back to his wife's; in countries where two, three, five or seven helms are seen on a shield, those on the dexter are turned and face those on the sinister.

It is also found in the arms of great lords bringing together provinces or kingdoms: when two lions sit side by side, the dexter one sometimes turns out of courtesy to the province shown by the sinister lion — more rarely in a quarterly than in a per pale. Another case, the old coat of arms of Alsace: two coats of arms, each bearing a bend, had to be combined there, and placing them side by side without reversal would have been ungainly; hence the reversal of that of Lower Alsace, the bend becoming a bend sinister. The arms of Alsace-Lorraine, likewise, combine those of Upper and Lower Alsace turned towards those of Lorraine.

Courtesy isn't the artist's only freedom: the size of certain charges or of the shield can also be changed. The alerions of the Montmorency went, over the centuries, from four to sixteen; to fit them into the quarterly, those of quarters 3 and 4 had to be reduced so as to appear in full.

XIII.4

Quarters and greater arms: a shield to tell a genealogy

437 words

Quarters result from a certain number of lines per pale and per fess (from two to six), marking out from six to thirty-six elementary arms. They are numbered in reading order: starting at the top left, then left to right, then the following rows. One first blazons “per pale of two, per fess of three” (six quarters), then each coat with its number; a repeated coat is stated at its first occurrence (“first, third and fifth…”) and quarters already described are skipped.

Each quarter stands for its holder — a family, a fief, a kingdom. It should therefore rather be blazoned by naming that holder than by describing its arms; if the holder is little known, it is better to give both. A textbook example: the arms of Lothar Franz von Schönborn, per pale of two lines and per fess of one, with six quarters of which the first and sixth are identical, as are the second and fifth, and an escutcheon overall.

The exercise can be pushed to artifice. The famous “Grenville diptych”, composed for Richard Temple-Grenville, lines up all the quarters of a genealogy: it is no longer the emblem of a person, but a bundle of alliances and a genealogical claim, close to a personal armorial. The language of blazon could describe such images point by point (per pale of 256, per fess of 256…); taken to the extreme, this excess clearly leaves the realm of heraldry.

Greater arms (grandes armes) are coats of arms adorned with all their external ornaments. They usually take the form of a shield composed of quarters, showing the arms of dependencies, parts or possessions, with the shield of the owner — a prince, a province… — over all. A solemn assertion of titles and rights, they are hardly shown except to adorn a state room, a throne room for example. The page Lineages presents some, for several dynasties, reign by reign.

Another way of composing is to add to the main arms an honourable ordinary — usually a chief or a canton: this is an augmentation. In France, the bonnes villes, which had the right to be represented by their mayors at the king's coronation, could bear on their arms a chief of France, “azure semé of fleurs-de-lis or” or “azure, three fleurs-de-lis or”: the chief of France is an augmentation of arms granted to these towns. The rule of tincture is often not respected there, and the chief is said to be “sewn” (cousu). England also granted augmentations, in the 18th century, notably to soldiers, to record their successes.

XIII.5

The obiit: the arms of mourning

183 words

An obiit (from the Latin obivit, “he died”) is the name usage gives to funerary arms; English calls it a hatchment. It is a painting or illumination, usually on a lozenge-shaped wooden panel, with a dark, often black ground, showing the year of death — sometimes also of birth — and the person's arms. The ground is white for a young woman who died unmarried.

The shape of the coat of arms states the status of the deceased woman: the obiit of a wife, or a widow, bears her oval coat of arms beside her husband's; a lozenge corresponds to a single woman. In the United Kingdom, the ground is sometimes divided vertically into white and black: black on the left and white on the right, it is a dead husband leaving a widow; white on the left and black on the right, a dead married woman leaving a widower.

The obiit is placed before the catafalque during the funeral ceremony, then brought back to the castle, or fixed to an interior wall of the church with which the family was linked.

XIV.1

The herald: tournament crier, then expert in arms

289 words

A herald (héraut, or herald of arms) is an officer of the office of arms, charged with making certain solemn proclamations or carrying important messages; he is usually recognised by the tabard he wears. The office has four grades: the king of arms, who oversees a march of arms; the marshal of arms, who assists him and can replace him; the herald proper; and the pursuivant, an apprentice herald. They are the ones who give heraldry its name.

Heralds probably appeared in the 12th century — a mention by Chrétien de Troyes dates from the end of that century — and are closely linked to the development of heraldry. Coming from the ranks of jugglers and minstrels, they specialised in tournaments, jousts and pas d'armes: they announced them, led the knights into them and commented on them. At first itinerant, attached to no particular lord, they contributed to the renown of knights; in the 14th century, great lords took several into their service, because the more one had, the more one displayed one's power.

The whole organisation of the corps bears the mark of tournaments: the officers' marches of arms correspond to the territorial divisions of knightly groups at tournaments, and it took a pursuivant seven years to become a herald — the time it took a squire to become a knight, as Olivier de la Marche recalls in his memoirs.

A historical point: attempts have been made to link heralds to the kerykes of the Greeks and the fetiales of the Romans. Nothing proves this link; it seems to have been created in the 15th century by the heralds themselves, to prove the antiquity and nobility of an office then under challenge.

XIV.2

In France: from tournament arms to the judge of arms

398 words

Experts in arms thanks to their role in tournaments, officers of arms received official military functions at the beginning of the 14th century, as shown by Philip the Fair's ordinance of 1306 on the wager of battle. There was no uniform in the feudal host: men recognised one another only by the arms on banners, pennons or shields, and heralds, who knew them, quickly identified the protagonists and grasped the course of battles — just as they recognised, by their colours, the knights fallen on the field. They also acquired new skills: defining the rules of heraldry and composing armorials.

The 15th century was both their golden age and their crisis. The French heraldic college was formed in 1406; in January 1407, a chapel of the kings of arms and heralds of the realm was founded at the Petit-Saint-Antoine in Paris, making them a confraternity. But the right granted to the humblest captain to retain a pursuivant multiplied the recruits, sometimes judged unworthy (« de vielz menestrels qui ne poient plus corner », “old minstrels who can no longer blow a horn”, says the herald Sicily), and, from 1445, the paid companies of ordinance replaced the contingents of vassals rallied to their lord's banner.

The decline came above all in the 16th and 17th centuries: the modern state transferred dignities to the monarch and took away the nobility's military character. The heralds' heraldic role disappeared in 1615, with the creation of the judge of arms (see the chapter “The law of arms”); in 1627, their college lost its independence and was attached to the Great Stable; Louis XIII was the last King of France to surround himself with heralds at war. The office, reduced to an element of monarchical and imperial pomp, survived until 1830: officers of arms appear at the opening of the Estates General of 1789, at the coronation of Napoleon in 1804, at the funeral of Louis XVIII, at the coronation of Charles X in 1825 — and, the last mention in France, at the head of the procession of the Te Deum celebrating the capture of Algiers, on 11 July 1830.

Some monarchies still have heralds: England, where they took part in the coronation of Elizabeth in 1953, Canada, Scotland (the Lord Lyon: see the chapter “Elsewhere”), Spain, where they took part in the proclamation of Juan Carlos in 1975, and Ireland.

XIV.3

Armorials: registers of arms

344 words

An armorial is a register, table or dictionary that matches names of families or communities with coats of arms; some are illustrated, others are limited to the blazon. The first arms appeared around 1130; about fifty years passed before the first collections were produced. Nearly a million medieval coats of arms (before the 16th century) are thus known, on rolls, notebooks and registers, but also on walls, sculptures, stained glass…

They are classified in several ways. First, by nature:

  • occasional armorials, the oldest, drawn up on the occasion of an event — tournament, marriage, treaty, campaign — to describe the arms of those present: the Bigot Roll (1254, of which a copy survives: the knights of Charles of Anjou's campaign in Hainaut), the armorial of the Compiègne tournament (1278), the Falkirk Roll (1298), the Wijnbergen (13th century), Bellenville (around 1372) and Gelre (around 1430, very famous) armorials;
  • provincial armorials, such as the Balliol Roll (1332) or the Zürich Roll (around 1340), then institutional ones: the idea is to list the arms of a whole kingdom, or the members of a confraternity or an order of chivalry (the Great Equestrian Armorial of the Golden Fleece); it was then that courts created the office of judges of arms, the most famous being those of the d'Hozier family;
  • general armorials, which record all the arms of a territory, up to Rietstap's Armorial général, which brought together for the first time, in a single work, hundreds of armorials from across Europe — and armorials of noble families.

They are also distinguished by author — an official with access to reliable and exhaustive documents, or an amateur dependent on the goodwill of informants — and by arrangement: by the bearer's name, or by content, which allows the bearer to be found from the arms (an “ordinary of arms”; the armorial of the lion is an example).

The Armorial général de France of 1696 (see the chapter “The law of arms”) holds a special place: it aimed at the tax office more than at memory.

XV.1

The bonnes villes and their chief of France

246 words

A bonne ville (“good town”) is, in the France of the Ancien Régime, a town enjoying privileges and protections granted by the king, with the obligation to contribute to the royal levy by providing a contingent of men-at-arms. The expression is attested in diplomatics from the 12th century; according to Robert Favreau, whom authors now follow, the English chancery was the first to use it — a letter of 25 July 1209 bears the first known occurrence. The status grew in importance at the end of the Middle Ages, to the point that towns not enjoying it fought to obtain it.

In heraldry, the bonnes villes had the right to bear arms with “a chief azure bearing three fleurs-de-lis or”, the arms of France: this is the chief of France, an augmentation (see the chapter “Alliances”). Some, like Marseille, Rennes or Avignon, chose not to include it in their arms.

The notion revived in the 19th century. Under the Second Restoration, an ordinance of 23 April 1821 definitively fixed the list of forty bonnes villes and their order of precedence; they had the privilege of sending their mayor to the king's coronation — which actually happened at the coronation of Charles X, on 29 May 1825 — and of putting a chief with fleurs-de-lis in their arms. The July Monarchy, in 1830, sought to exchange the fleurs-de-lis for three gold stars, but this usage was short-lived; it briefly reappeared under the Second Republic, in 1848.

XV.2

1809: the Empire ranks towns in three orders

397 words

The Revolution abolished the custom of the chief of France, and many towns lost arms bearing the signs of royalty. Under the First Empire, a new, largely honorific status of bonne ville was recreated: towns whose mayors had the right and duty to attend the Emperor's swearing of the oath. The sénatus-consulte of 18 May 1804 provided for thirty-six; a decree of 22 June 1804 fixed their list and order of precedence; their mayors attended the coronation of Napoleon, on 2 December 1804. The decree of 1 March 1808 gave the mayors of bonnes villes, after ten years in office, the life title of baron. Annexations brought the number to fifty — thirty French towns and twenty foreign ones, from Genoa to Hamburg and from Rome to Amsterdam.

The decree of 17 May 1809 gave towns back the right to obtain arms: in future, neither town nor corporation could take any without the Emperor's authorisation. It divided municipalities into three orders, and fixed for each a mark in the shield and ornaments:

  • bonnes villes (“duchesses”) bear a chief gules charged with three bees or in fess, with a mural crown of seven crenellations, topped by an eagle or, rising, supported by a caduceus or from which hang two festoons, of oak and olive;
  • towns of the second order (“countesses”), whose mayors, like those of the bonnes villes, are appointed by the Emperor, bear a quarter to dexter azure, charged with an N or surmounted by a radiant star, with a mural crown of five crenellations argent;
  • towns of the third order (“baronesses”), whose mayors are appointed by the prefects, bear a quarter to sinister gules charged with the same figures argent, and a basket of sheaves or as a crest.

This is the logic of 1808 applied to towns (see the article “1808 — rank enters the shield”): rank is read in the shield — chief, quarter — and in the ornaments, where the mural crown takes the place of the coronets of duke, count or baron. The arms of Rouen were granted on 2 November 1810; those of Orléans, Metz, Nantes and Marseille on the 21st; those of Lyon on 19 January 1811; those of Paris on the 29th.

The Second Restoration kept the dignity for the twenty-nine bonnes villes of the Empire located in France, and added eleven from 1816 to 1821.

XV.3

The mural crown

125 words

The mural crown, also called towered, is an honorific attribute fitted with towers, symbolising the walls of a fortified town. In Greco-Roman antiquity, it crowns the brow of the protective deities of cities — the Hellenistic period often links it with the goddess Cybele-Tyche. In the Roman army, it rewarded the first soldier over an enemy wall: the tribune Lucius Siccius Dentatus, nicknamed the “Roman Achilles”, is said to have received it three times. It personifies Italy under the name “Italia turrita”.

In European heraldry, it adorns the arms of many municipalities. The Empire made it the sign of towns' rank, with seven or five crenellations according to their order (see the previous article); for the other coronets of rank, see the chapter “Ornaments”.

XV.4

Canting arms: when the shield makes a pun

405 words

Canting arms (armes parlantes, “speaking arms”) include figures that more or less fully express the bearer's name. The evocation works by ear: it was originally addressed to an audience that, more often than not, couldn't read, and that recognised the name in the words of the blazon spoken by the herald at tournaments. A more intellectual tendency produced allusive arms, where the name is no longer directly heard but only evoked. English keeps the old term of “singing” arms: canting arms.

The methods vary:

  • directly: a cock for a “Lecoq”, a lion for León, a castle for Castile;
  • by homonym: a pomegranate (grenade) for the city of Granada;
  • by near-sound: a chalice (calice) for Galicia, a bush (buisson) for Aubusson, a grass snake (Latin coluber) for Colbert;
  • by etymology: an arm holding a spear for Shakespeare, an ox with its feet in water for Oxford (“ox ford”);
  • as a rebus: a castle (château) and a fox (renard) for Châteaurenard, a bear (ours) and two cats (chats) for Chaource, a boar and a fess wavy azure for Eberbach (Eber, boar; Bach, stream), a rat and a swan (cygne) for Racine;
  • a mix of rebus and near-sound: a hinge (gond) and an S for Gonesse, flowers (fleurs) in a bag (sac) for Florensac, a wing (aile) and a bone (os) for Allos.

At one remove, allusive arms only bring to mind words that evoke the name: a boar, more flattering, for someone named “Cochon” (pig); a sailing ship for Marines. They are very common in the arms of guilds, where the object worked or the tool stands in for the name of the trade. The arms of the Forcadel family mix both levels: the oak symbolises strength (force), the greyhound bitch faithfulness (fidèle), and the start and end of the surname echo “force” and “fidèle”.

The Armorial général of 1696 made a less kindly use of them: the clerks who assigned arms to the recalcitrant first composed canting arms (see the article “1696 — the edict that taxed coats of arms”). The Wikipedia article refers, for further reading, to Michel Pastoureau (“Une écriture en images : les armoiries parlantes”, 2008), whom we haven't read.

Couchant (couché)
Said of an animal with its body stretched out, the head remaining raised (for a stag, gisant); when the head also rests on the ground, it is dormant.
Counterchanged (de l'un en l'autre)
Said of a figure over a field shared between two alternating tinctures: each part of the figure takes the tincture opposite to the ground it covers. This is the definition Wikipedia adopts for lack of consensus: authors distinguishing it from de l'un à l'autre don't agree on the sense of each, and Galbreath and Jéquier consider the distinction a chinoiserie.
Couped (alésé)
Said of a shortened honourable ordinary that no longer touches the edges of the shield: “argent, a cross couped gules”. It then behaves like a charge. The Atelier can draw the fess, pale, bend, bend sinister, chevron and saltire couped; its couped cross is a charge.
Courant
Said of a quadruped shown at a gallop: the two hind legs on the ground, the body horizontal. A stag in this attitude is rather called élancé.
Cow (vache)
Recognised by its udder; generally passant, the tail stretched along the flank. The arms of Béarn bear two: “or, two cows gules, horned, collared and belled azure”.
Crescent (croissant)
A moon figure; horns up by default, tourné, contourné or versé according to their direction.
Crest (cimier)
A figure placed on top of the helm, above the shield.
Cross (croix)
An honourable ordinary formed by the meeting of the pale and the fess.
Cross formy, pattée (croix pattée)
A cross whose arms flare at the ends.
Cross moline, anchored cross (croix ancrée)
A cross whose arms end in anchor flukes.
Crozier (crosse)
A pastoral staff with a volute, symbol of the Good Shepherd and of jurisdiction; its heraldic use on episcopal arms was forbidden in 1969.
D
Dancetty (dancetté)
A zigzag line with few large teeth.
Dexter (dextre)
The bearer's right side — and so the viewer's left.
Dexter arm, sinister arm (dextrochère, senestrochère)
The arm, right (dextrochère) or left (senestrochère). Bare by default, it can be clothed (paré) or armoured (armé), and often issues from a flank, which must then be blazoned.
Dimidiation (mi-parti)
The union of two coats of arms by the dexter half of one and the sinister half of the other. Not to be confused with coupé mi-parti, which the Wikipedia page advises avoiding.
Displayed (éployée)
The eagle's usual position, wings spread on either side.
Divise
A diminished fess, used alone on its field (also spelt devise). Reduced by half according to one entry in Wikipedia's list, to less than a third according to another; its default place is that of the chief for Charles de Grandmaison, that of the fess for Pierre Palliot, who calls it divise en chef when it is at the top.
Double-headed (bicéphale)
With two heads; said above all of the imperial eagle.
E
Embattled (crénelé)
With square crenellations, like the top of a wall.
Engrailed (engrêlé)
Edged with small arcs whose points bite outwards.
Enquire arms (armes à enquerre)
Arms deliberately breaking the rule of tincture, which invites one to “enquire” into their history.
Ermine (hermine)
A fur of argent semé of sable spots.
Escutcheon (écusson)
A small shield of arms placed within the shield (“argent, charged with an escutcheon gules”). Set at the heart of a quarterly shield, it is called “overall”.
Étai
A common synonym of chevronel; for L.-A. Duhoux d'Argicourt, a small chevron supporting a charge, a view Wikipedia considers a minority one. Sometimes used in the feminine: une étaie.
F
Fess (fasce)
A horizontal honourable ordinary across the middle of the shield.
Fess point (abîme)
The exact centre of the shield; a single figure placed there is said to be en abîme.
Field (champ)
The background of the shield, on which ordinaries and charges are placed.
Filet
A much diminished bendlet, set fesswise, palewise, bendwise or bendwise sinister — always stated. The filet en croix, or étrez, is a cross reduced to a filet.
Filière
A bordure whose thickness is reduced to a filet.
Fimbriated (bordé)
Said of an ordinary surrounded by a border of a different colour, a narrow edging: “a cross argent fimbriated gules”.
Flank (flanc)
An honourable ordinary corresponding to the dexter or sinister edge of the shield and extending to a third of it; rare.
Flanked (accosté)
Said of an elongated figure — set palewise, bendwise or bendwise sinister — with other figures beside it, turned in the same direction. A round figure (bezant, ring) isn't flanked; if the neighbouring figures are turned differently, French says accompagné, and côtoyé when they are numerous.
Fleur-de-lis (fleur de lis)
A stylised lily with three petals bound by a band; emblem of the kings of France.
Flighted, barbed, shafted (empenné, ferré, fûté; arrow)
An arrow consists of a shaft, a point and feathers. When the point is a different colour, it is armed or barbed (armée, ferrée); when the feathers are, it is flighted (empennée); when the shaft is, it is fûtée. Set alone, it is palewise, point up; point down, it is versée.
Franc-canton
A square set in a corner, slightly larger than the canton and smaller than the quarter, in dexter chief by default; a third of the shield's width according to the values most often cited.
Fur (fourrure)
A tincture shown by a conventional pattern: ermine, vair and their variants.
Fusily (fuselé)
A field covered with abutting fusils (elongated lozenges), that is, touching at their points: an abutting semé of fusils, according to the page “Champ composé”. Called en bande or en barre, the fusils are set in that direction and each row traces the other diagonal. The Atelier draws a fusily of fixed proportions.
G
Galero
A broad-brimmed ecclesiastical hat ensigning the arms of clergy; its colour states the nature of the office, its tassels the dignity.
Greater arms (grandes armes)
Coats of arms adorned with all their external ornaments, generally a shield composed of quarters showing dependencies, with the owner's shield over all: a solemn assertion of titles and rights.
Griffin (griffon)
A chimerical creature combining the front of an eagle with the back of a lion.
Gules (gueules)
Colour tincture, red; vertical hatching in engraving.
Gusset (gousset)
An ordinary formed, at the top, of a triangle issuing from the chief and joining, below, a pale. Its name comes from a piece of cloth, then from the metal piece linking the front and back of a suit of armour.
Gyron (giron)
A right-angled triangle whose base is half of one side of the field and whose opposite angle is at the centre of the shield; by default, the one issuing from the upper half of the dexter flank. Its shapes are those of a gyronny of eight.
Gyronny (gironné)
A field divided into gyrons — triangles, usually eight, meeting at the centre.
H
Hamaide
An ordinary formed of three couped fesses with rounded or chamfered ends, held to be a single ordinary: “or, a hamaide gules”, arms of the Lords of La Hamaide (Belgium).
Heart (cœur)
The centre of the shield (see Fess point).
Herald (héraut)
An officer of the office of arms, charged with solemn proclamations and messages, wearing the tabard, expert in arms. Four grades: king of arms, marshal of arms, herald, pursuivant.
Heraldic courtesy (courtoisie héraldique)
A change to a coat of arms made for balance or harmony: turning the husband's lion to look at the wife's arms, reversing a bend into a bend sinister, reducing a charge to fit its quarter.
Honourable ordinary (pièce honorable)
A large geometric figure crossing the shield (fess, pale, chevron, cross…).
I
In saltire (passées en sautoir)
Said of two keys (or swords, spears) crossed in an X; on papal arms, one is or, the other argent.
Indented (denché)
In large triangular teeth; dancetty has only three or four, very prominent.
Interlaced (entretenues)
Said of two keys whose rings are intertwined.
Invected (cannelé)
Like engrailed, but with the small arcs reversed (also called invecté).
Issuant (issant)
Said of an animal of which only the upper part is seen, as if emerging from an ordinary.
Issuing (mouvant)
Said of a figure that starts from a point or edge of the shield (chief, flank…) without occupying its full length.
L
Label (lambel)
A mark of difference shaped like a bar with points, the mark of the eldest son during his father's lifetime.
Langued (lampassé)
Said of an animal whose tongue is a different tincture.
Leopard (léopard)
In French, a lion shown passant with its head facing out (lion passant guardant) — a question of posture, not species.
Line (trait)
A line dividing the field; its direction (per pale, per fess…) and shape (wavy, engrailed…) are blazoned.
Lord Lyon
Lord Lyon King of Arms, Scotland's king of arms: he grants new arms, matriculates old ones and judges, on prosecution by a procurator fiscal, the illegal use of arms.
M
Mantle (manteau)
A coloured drapery, generally lined with ermine (with vair for Dukes of the Empire), falling around the shield: an attribute of the sovereign, princes and dukes. That of the peers of France was azure. Drawn with the holder's arms on its side parts, or in the colours of the shield.
Mantling (lambrequins)
Foliate cut-outs framing the shield, derived from the helm's veil.
Mark of difference (brisure)
A slight change to arms to distinguish a branch or younger son of a family.
Martlet (merlette)
A stylised blackbird without beak or legs, often used as a mark of cadency.
Matriculation (immatriculation)
In Scotland, the entry of existing arms in the public register, after distinguishing them if need be with a difference; a judicial act of the Lord Lyon, open to appeal.
Membered (membré)
Said of a bird whose legs are a different tincture; beaked (becqué) for the beak.
Mitre
The two-pointed headdress of bishops and mitred abbots; removed from Catholic episcopal arms by the instruction of 31 March 1969, kept in the Church of England.
Motto (devise)
A short phrase suggesting an ideal, a rule of conduct or a glorious past; in heraldry, usually on a scroll beneath the shield.
Mural crown (couronne murale)
A crown fitted with towers, symbol of the walls of a fortified town; the Empire made it, with seven or five crenellations, the sign of towns' rank.
N
Nebuly (nébulé)
In rounded scallops suggesting clouds.
O
Obiit, hatchment
Funerary arms, usually painted on a lozenge-shaped panel with a dark ground, with the year of death; an oval coat of arms for a wife or widow, a lozenge for a single woman.
Or
Metal tincture representing yellow; dotted in engraving.
Orle
A narrowed bordure detached from the edge of the shield.
Overall (sur le tout)
Said alone, followed by a tincture, this is short for “an escutcheon over all”: a small shield set at the centre of the main shield, on top of the rest, and read last. An escutcheon placed in turn on the first is “over all of all”. For another figure, one writes “a [charge] over all” (brochant sur le tout). Not to be confused with en abîme (a single figure at the central point, directly on the field) nor with “over all” for a figure passing across the whole.
P
Pale (pal)
A vertical honourable ordinary down the middle of the shield.
Pall (pairle)
A Y-shaped ordinary: the lower part of the pale and the upper halves of the bend and bend sinister, joined at the centre of the shield — three arms reaching the two corners of the chief and the base. Shortened and narrowed (“couped and pointed”), it corresponds to the shakefork of British heraldry, which has no name of its own in French.
Pallet (vergette)
A pale reduced in width, repeated five times or more.
Pallium
A circular band of white wool charged with black crosses, characteristic of the arms of metropolitan archbishops.
Paly (palé)
A field covered with alternating pales (a multiplied per pale).
Passant
The attitude of an animal walking horizontally, its paws on the ground.
Pavilion (pavillon)
A tent topping the mantle and setting it further apart: reserved for the sovereign alone, it marks the summit of the hierarchy.
Per bend (tranché)
A shield divided by the diagonal from dexter chief to sinister base.
Per bend sinister (taillé)
A shield divided by the diagonal from sinister chief to dexter base.
Per fess (coupé)
A shield divided horizontally into two equal parts.
Per pale (parti)
A shield divided vertically into two equal parts.
Pile
An isosceles triangle whose base, two thirds of the shield's width, hangs from the chief and whose point aims downwards without reaching the bottom. Bendwise or bendwise sinister, it issues from a corner of the chief.
Plain (plain, plein)
Said of a shield of a single tincture, with no charge or ordinary: “argent plain” (more rarely champé d'argent). When only one part of a division is plain, the word can be left out. The Atelier writes plein.
Plaine
A diminished champagne.
Processional cross (croix de procession)
A staffed cross set palewise behind the shield — one crossbar for a bishop, two for an archbishop, primate and patriarch.
Proper (au naturel)
Said of a charge shown in its real colour, outside the tinctures of heraldry; two special cases have their own name, carnation for flesh and tenné (tanné) for the brown of leather. Reserved for realistic charges — never the field, ordinaries or geometric charges.
Purpure (pourpre)
Colour tincture, violet; diagonals from sinister chief to dexter base.
Q
Quarter (franc-quartier)
A quarter placed in the dexter corner of the chief; under the Empire it bears the mark of rank (azure for counts, gules for barons) and of office.
Quarter (quartier)
Each of the divisions of a divided shield — and, by extension, the arms it bears. They are numbered in reading order: top before bottom, dexter before sinister.
Quarterly (écartelé)
A shield divided into four quarters by a vertical and a horizontal line.
R
Rampant
Said of an animal standing in profile on one leg, in an attacking posture.
Ranged in (rangé en)
Said of several charges lined up in the direction of the ordinary named next: in fess (horizontally), in pale (vertically), in bend or in bend sinister (diagonally). The alignment doesn't set the position of each charge: three keys “ranged in fess” are lined up horizontally, each remaining palewise. Rangé en chef sums up “ranged in fess” placed in chief.
Rearing (cabré)
Said of a horse standing almost vertically on its hind legs. Effaré and effrayé are synonyms (some authors add forcené); effaré extends to other animals, but for the horse cabré is preferred.
Repetition (rebattement)
Repetition of a simple division or ordinary over the whole field, giving striped fields (paly, barry…) and certain honourable ordinaries: “or, three pales gules”. Joubert, followed by this site's glossary, rather uses the word for the diminutive of a narrowed ordinary (barrulet, pallet, bendlet…); common usage calls that a diminished ordinary.
Right of precedence (droit d'antériorité)
A customary rule settling conflicts of arms in favour of the earliest bearer; formed under the Ancien Régime, it still holds.
Rose
A flower with five petals, with its barbs (sepals) and seeds at the heart.
Rule of tincture (règle des émaux)
The ban on placing metal on metal or colour on colour, for legibility.
S
Sable
Colour tincture, black; crossed hatching in engraving.
Saltire (sautoir)
A cross set as an X, formed by the two diagonals.
Scroll (listel)
A ribbon or band bearing the motto or war cry, usually beneath the shield, sometimes above or around it. The French word also, more rarely, means the bordure.
Sejant (assis)
Said of an animal whose hindquarters rest on the ground and whose body is upright, its forepaws also on the ground; raised, they make it accroupi.
Semé
Said of a field (of a shield, division or ordinary) in which a small charge is repeated many times, in no fixed number: the charges are drawn incomplete near the edges, as if continuing beyond, or disappear beneath the figures covering them. The field and the semé charges obey the rule of tincture: “azure semé of fleurs-de-lis or” (France ancient).
Sewn (cousu)
Said of an ordinary — above all the chief — of metal on metal or colour on colour, held to be sewn to the field rather than placed on it, so as to escape the rule of tincture. Authors differ: Palliot (1664) extends the word to the other honourable ordinaries, Rietstap (1887) considers it useless, the fault being plain to see.
Shield (écu)
The shield, the support of a coat of arms; its shape doesn't enter the blazon.
Ship (navire)
The basic ship has a simple hull, a mast and a sail; it can be rigged, its sails filled (“azure, a ship argent”). The oared ship (longship, drakkar) represents royal power in ancient Scottish heraldry; the galley has sails that are always furled.
Sinister (senestre)
The bearer's left side — and so the viewer's right.
Spur rowel (molette)
The rowel of a spur: similar to the star but pierced at its centre.
Star (étoile)
A radiant figure, with five rays in French usage, six in English usage.
Stringed (cordé)
Said of a bow whose string is a different tincture; the arms of Ireland likewise describe the harp as “or stringed argent”.
Supporters (supports, tenants)
Figures (animals, beings) holding up the shield on either side; in French, tenants if human.
Surmounted (surmonté)
Said of a figure bearing another figure above it: a Noah's ark “surmounted by a house”, a building “surmounted by a weathervane” (it is then girouetté).
Suspended arms (armoiries sursises)
Arms held back for checking in 1696, notably those bearing fleurs-de-lis or on a field azure, subject to authorisation by the order of 22 July 1698.
T
Tassel (houppe)
The tuft ending the cord of the ecclesiastical hat; the higher the dignity, the more tassels.
Terrassé
Said of a tree planted on a terrace, normally issuing from the base of the shield.
Tierce
A group of three trangles separated by a space equal to their width, the whole as thick as a fess; it is blazoned like the bar gemel.
Tierced (tiercé)
A field divided into three equal parts of three tinctures (in pale, in fess or in bend).
Timbre
What surmounts the shield (helm, crown, mitre…) and marks rank.
Tincture (émail)
The generic name for the hues of heraldry: metals, colours and furs.
Tire
A horizontal row of repeated pieces in a composite field (chequy, vairy, semé): échiqueté de six tires.
To blazon (blasonner)
To describe a coat of arms in the technical language and agreed order of heraldry.
To divide further (recouper)
To divide an existing division again; great shields are composed by such further divisions.
Topped (sommé)
Said of a figure bearing another figure at its summit (“an anchor topped by a crescent”, “a bridge topped by a castle”). For a stag, antlers of a particular tincture are called ramée or sommée, and sommé de dix cors counts its points.
Toque
A black velvet headdress with a plume which, in Imperial heraldry, replaces the coronet of rank for all except the imperial family.
Torteau (tourteau)
A solid round figure in a colour — the counterpart of the bezant, which is of metal.
Trangle
A diminished fess always found in odd numbers. Alone, it is rather called a divise; in even numbers, barrulets.
Tusked, eyed (défendu, allumé; boar's head)
For a boar's head, if the tusk is a different tincture, it is tusked (défendue de…); if the eye, it is eyed (allumée de…).
U
Usurpation
Taking another's arms — the only truly general ban in heraldic law; everything else is free.
V
Vair
A fur shown by alternating bell shapes of argent and azure.
Vergeté
Said of a field divided palewise into more than eight pieces (pallets), as paly has fewer.
Vert (sinople)
Colour tincture, green; diagonals from dexter chief to sinister base.
Vivré
A line in a sharp zigzag, with re-entrant and salient angles.
W
War cry (cri de guerre)
A rallying word or phrase of an army or company; in heraldry, it appears above the shield — the motto going below.
Wavy (ondé)
Said of a line or edge drawn in gentle waves.
Wings lowered (vol abaissé)
Said of a bird whose wings, away from the body, have their feathers pointing downwards: an eagle with wings lowered.
Wyvern
A two-legged dragon, which British heraldry distinguishes from the four-legged dragon. The word comes from the Anglo-French wivre, “viper”, like the French guivre.

Lyon King of Arms Act 1592 (1592 c. 29) et Lyon King of Arms Act 1672 (1672 c. 47)

Écosse (Parlement du royaume)
legislation.gov.uk — revised text, The National Archives, 1592 and 1672 (texts accessed in 2026)
Legal texts
Our reading

Original texts in Scots, read in their revised version. The first gives the Lord Lyon the power to visit, difference and matriculate arms, with confiscation and a fine for usurpation; the second ratifies this, creates the public register of arms of Scotland and sets the fine at a hundred pounds per offence. Parts repealed in 1906, a few words in 1987 and 1995; both acts remain in force.
