Why shields change

The transmission of arms

Arms do not change at random: a cadet adds a mark, a marriage brings in an heiress's quarters, a treaty adds a crown. Unfold the tree — each passage says what changes in the shield, and why.

Method & state of knowledgewhat this page asserts, and what it leaves open

The tree starts from eleven families (Capetians, Habsburg, Castile, England, Scotland, Savoy, Nassau, Oldenburg, Wettin, Wittelsbach, Lorraine), chosen because they carry a real line of descent; it is growing little by little. A node is a state of arms, not a person; a link is a passage of arms. Blazons, figures and credits come from the Lineages pages, the “Armorial des Capétiens” (French Wikipedia) or, for the Nassaus, Oldenburgs, Wettins, Wittelsbachs and Lorraines, their own armorial: nothing is copied here. A house with no branch of its own — Aragon, Delmenhorst — has no card: it appears as a “with” chip under the arms it fed, where its marriage or union changes them.

The reasons (“Why”) are reworded from what was read: the milestones of the timelines, the presentation of each branch in the “Armorial des Capétiens” and several Wikipedia articles (cited under each link), mostly in French; “the page read” below means one of these. When the sources read give the fact but not the reason, the link says so — “reason not established” — instead of guessing. When they contradict each other, the disagreement is flagged.

The tree follows one main line per node (that of the house the arms come from); other contributions — a marriage, an inheritance from elsewhere — are secondary links, listed under “How these arms came about” and clickable. This is not a genealogy: it follows not people, only the path of shields.
